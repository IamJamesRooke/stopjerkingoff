# A City Without Walls

> "Like a city that is broken down and without walls is a man whose spirit is without restraint."
>
> — Proverbs 25:28

The king was David. His story is very old, and it's about you.

Start with that proverb. It comes from King Solomon, David's son. In his day a city's wall was the city. Inside it were homes, markets, kids playing and a temple. Knock the wall down and you still have all of it, until the first raiding party shows up. A city without walls belongs to whoever arrives, however rich it is.

Solomon says a man without self-control is that city. He doesn't call him wicked. He calls him defenseless. Whatever shows up walks in: a mood, an image, a bad day, a pretty face on a screen. That man doesn't get a vote on his own evening.

## The king and his soldier {-}

Solomon knew what he was talking about. His mother was a woman named Bathsheba, and this is how his parents met.

David had been a soldier long before he was a king. One year, at the season when kings went to war, he sent his army out and stayed home. One evening he walked on the palace roof and saw a woman bathing. He asked who she was. The answer should have ended it. She was the wife of Uriah.

David knew that name. The Bible keeps a list of David's mighty men, the fighters who'd stood beside him for years, and Uriah's name is on it. That night Uriah was at the front, fighting David's war.

David sent for her anyway and slept with her. Nobody asked what she wanted. He was the king. Some weeks later she sent him a message: "I am with child."

So David called Uriah home. He wanted him to spend a night with his wife, so the child would pass as his. But Uriah wouldn't go home. He slept at the palace door with the servants. When David asked why, Uriah said his commander and his brothers were camped in the open field. "Shall I then go into my house to eat and to drink, and to lie with my wife? As you live, and as your soul lives, I will not do this thing!"

The next night David got him drunk. Even drunk, Uriah had more honor than the king had sober. He slept at the door again.

In the morning David wrote a letter to his general. "Send Uriah to the forefront of the hottest battle, and retreat from him, that he may be struck, and die." He gave the letter to Uriah to carry. The man walked back to the war with his own death warrant in his hand and, as far as the Bible tells us, handed it over unopened. Loyal to the end.

The general did as he was told. Uriah died under the city wall, and other soldiers died with him. When the news reached David, he sent back a shrug: "the sword devours one as well as another." Then he married the widow.

Look at what lust made of him. A soldier is supposed to die before he lets down the man beside him. David had that man killed to keep a secret. He got there one step at a time, and the first step was a look from a roof.

It stayed hidden until a prophet named Nathan came to the king with a story. A rich man with many flocks had taken a poor man's one pet lamb and cooked it for a guest. David was furious and said the man deserved to die. Nathan answered, "You are the man."

David didn't argue. He said, "I have sinned against the LORD." And Nathan told him, "The LORD also has put away your sin." Tradition says David wrote Psalm 51 after that day. It begins, "Have mercy on me, God, according to your loving kindness."

He was forgiven. But the child died, and David's family never knew peace again.

Uriah trusted his king, and his king was a city without walls.

## Your own word {-}

David broke faith with a man who trusted him. The man you've broken faith with most often is yourself.

Count your promises. "Never again." "This is the last time." "Starting Monday." You meant every one, maybe hundreds of times, and you broke every one. Each was a promise to yourself.

Break your word to yourself often enough and you stop believing it. Then a man says "I'll start the business", or "I'll get fit", or "I'll be a better husband", and something inside quietly answers, "No, you won't. I know you." That's the deepest damage the habit does. It makes you a man whose word, in his own ears, is worth nothing.

David wasn't weak where the world could see. He had one gap in the wall. So do you.

Don't take that as a verdict. Walls can be rebuilt. A governor named Nehemiah found Jerusalem's walls in ruins and had them back up in fifty-two days. Yours will go up faster. For now, just look at the gap and admit it's there.

So far this has been one-sided. You've heard what the habit costs and what stopping gains. A fair book gives both sides, so here's the other one, in full: the advantages of the habit.
