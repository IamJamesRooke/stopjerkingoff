# A Master, Not a Habit {#a-master-not-a-habit}

When all the other explanations have collapsed, most men settle on this one: it's only a habit, and habits are hard to break.

It sounds like an answer, but it isn't one.

Are habits hard to break? You change them all the time. You move to a new house, and for two days your hand goes to the wrong wall for the light switch. By the weekend it doesn't. You rent a car in a country that drives on the opposite side, and after one nervous mile you're fine. You start a new job and your whole morning is rearranged within a week. People drop thirty-year habits with no drama at all, including habits they liked.

So why would this one be different? This habit leaves you restless when you skip it and ashamed when you don't. You would break a nightstand to be rid of it. And stopping asks nothing of you. You don't have to do a thing. You only have to not do one. Why can't you?

Because it isn't a habit.

Jesus had the right word for it. "Everyone who commits sin is the bondservant of sin," he said. A bondservant is a slave. Two thousand years before anyone studied addiction, he had already named it. A man with a habit is in charge of it. This thing is in charge of you. Call it sin, or addiction, or a trap. This book uses all three. It will go on saying "the habit" as well, because that is what men call it. But never forget what it is. IT'S A MASTER, NOT A HABIT.

And there is only one of him. What sends you to the bathroom is what opens the site. They look like separate habits. One master is behind both.

And the joke of it is his size. For years you have been jumping for a pipsqueak in your own back room. He can't make you do a thing. You obeyed because you thought he was paying.

The men who have gotten out reach for the same word without being told. One of them, eight months clean, looked back and counted. He wrote that he had been a slave to it since he was twelve years old. He was thirty.

That is why it seems so hard to "give up". The slave has come to believe his master is also his comforter. He thinks the pleasure is worth its price, and that the crutch holds him up. He thinks life without it would have a hole in the middle.

Now the good news. Once you see how the master holds you, and why you really reach for the habit, you'll stop. Just like that. Before long only two mysteries will be left. One is how it ever had a grip on you. The other is why you can't get your friends to see what you see.

## The quiet trap {-}

The trap is quiet. Your own flesh supplies the bait, and the world stands around telling you it isn't a trap at all.

A boy gets no warning. Cigarettes at least bring a cough, and a label on the box. His own hand brings no dealer, no price, no hangover and no smell on his clothes. No alarm bell rings. Why would one?

Later something does feel wrong: the first guilt, or the first prayer he doesn't quite want to finish. But the routine is already laid down. From then on he lives like every other man in the trap. He wonders why he keeps doing it, and every so often he tries to break out.

So whose fault is it? One man who got out said it well: "I would say it is our fault and not our fault at the same time". He's right on both counts. Nobody chooses to walk into the trap, but a man does have to choose to walk out.

And when does a man try to break out? He almost never decides to stop on a good day. He decides after an incident. He's nearly caught. Or a friend says something that lands. Or he looks at his wife and feels like a fraud. Or he's with a real woman and his body lets him down. So he quits on the very day he feels worst, which is the very day his old comforter is the thing he most wants. He has piled a second misery on the first one and thrown away the only remedy he knows.

A few days of that and his resolve goes soft. "I picked a bad week. I'll do it when things calm down. After finals. When I've got a girlfriend. Once I'm married." Then things do calm down, and with the pressure off, the reason to stop has gone as well. He drifts on until the next incident.

And each year the calm weeks seem fewer. He's left home, there's a job, rent, a wife, small children. Life, he decides, just gets more stressful as you go.

Not as much as he thinks. He's mixing up responsibility with stress. He wasn't carefree at fourteen. He was a bundle of nerves. What has grown is the habit's share of the load. It doesn't calm a man down. It winds him up, quietly, one guilty night at a time: another secret to keep, another promise broken, another morning started already behind. He blames the job.

## The two causes {-}

Stopping can be easy for any man. But first you need the facts about why you're still doing it, and they aren't the frightening kind. It comes down to two things:

1. **The flesh.**
2. **The world's lies.**

Take those two away, and nothing is left to hold you.

The men who have already walked out aren't missing a thing. They aren't gritting their teeth or looking back with longing. If anything they seem faintly puzzled that they ever bothered.

Before long you'll be one of them. Jesus said the truth makes a man free, and a few verses later he said it is the Son who does it. This book is about one small corner of that truth: the lies lust tells. If you're not a believer, you can still test those lies for yourself.

Take the flesh first. It is the smaller of the two, and it is not what you think it is.
