# Beware of Cutting Down

> "But put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh, for its lusts."
>
> — Romans 13:14

Sooner or later almost every man in the trap tries setting himself a limit.

Sometimes he means it as a stepping stone: "I'll ease off gradually, and then I'll stop." Sometimes he means it as a truce with the Little Monster: "I'll never be rid of you, so let's agree on terms." It nearly always follows a failed attempt to stop outright. A few days in, the man cracks, and the next morning he says something like this:

"Never again is too much to ask. So from now on it's Saturdays only. Or only in the shower. Or only from my own head, with no screen. If I can hold it there, I'll trim it further, and one day it'll just fade out."

You may be living on one of these deals already. "Only without porn" is a limit. So is "only the tame videos", and so is "I'll look, but I won't touch".

One man tried such a deal for Lent, the forty days before Easter when many Christians give something up. He dropped porn and kept masturbation. He found he couldn't do the one without thinking about the other. A smaller meal is still a meal, and the Little Monster is still being fed.

![](images/illustrations/cutting-down-one-crumb.png){.illustration width=80%}

A limit sounds like the grown-up option. It keeps plenty of men chained for life. Four things happen to the man who sets one.

1. **He pays twice.** He's still feeding the Little Monster, so the itch never dies. He also thinks about the habit more than he ever did, so the lies get more to work with.
2. **He wishes his life away.** Monday to Friday become a corridor he has to walk down. He's a grown man counting sleeps until Saturday.
3. **He carries the itch all week.** Before the limit, he gave in when the urge came, and the restlessness at least eased for a while. Now he drags it behind him six days out of seven. He gets short with his wife and kids and can't think why.
4. **Every time becomes "special".** Before, most times were automatic. He barely noticed them, and he certainly didn't treasure them. Now that he's waited six days, Saturday night feels like Christmas.

Look hard at number four, because the whole trick is in it. Most of the relief isn't in the act. It's the itch ending. The longer the itch nags, the bigger the relief, and the surer he is that he's enjoying something. He's turned a dull routine into a treat by making himself suffer for it first.

With a limit, the habit's always on offer, because Saturday's always coming. The Little Monster never hears that the kitchen has closed. He just learns the opening hours, and he sits by the door all week.

![](images/illustrations/cutting-down-opening-hours.png){.illustration width=80%}

The hard part of stopping was never the flesh. It's the lie that the habit is a comfort or a reward, and cutting down props that lie up. A man living by a limit ends up convinced the most precious thing in his week is five minutes on a Saturday night. He set out to shrink the habit, and he's made it the center of his calendar.

## Less is not less {-}

Many men believe that the less they do it, the less they'll want it.

The reverse is true. The wait makes it sweeter, and soon the old favorites aren't enough. He "needs" something a little stronger to make the long wait worth it. That's how a man who swore he'd only ever use his imagination ends up back on his phone.

You can test this without changing anything. Think back to the days you did it twice. How was the second time? Flat and pointless, more than likely. Now think of a time you'd gone ten days without and then gave in. Fireworks.

Same act. Same hand. The only thing that changed was how long you'd been kept waiting. If a thing were truly good, it'd still be good the second time. A second helping of mashed potatoes is still mashed potatoes.

Is the Saturdays-only man in control of anything? He spends six days out of seven in a quiet fight, and the seventh losing it by appointment. His logic doesn't hold, either. Either there's something real in the habit or there isn't. If there is, why wait a week for it? If there isn't, why do it at all?

## Willpower for life {-}

An appetite that's fed, even a little, comes back asking for more. It never asks for less. So to hold the line at once a week, a man has to push back against the pull every day until he dies.

**Cutting down means willpower for life.**

If you had that kind of willpower to spare, you'd have stopped years ago. Stopping completely is far easier, and it hurts far less.

A limit is indulgence with a schedule.

The same goes for every half-measure dressed up as progress. Edging is one. A man goes right up to the end and stops, and tells himself, "At least I didn't finish." He's kept the engine revving for an hour. Looking without touching is another. So is touching without looking. So is "just checking". They're all ways of cutting down. One man who'd tried edging left this for the men coming after him:

> "Don't edge. Really. Don't do it. It's the absolutely worst thing to do."

Under one man's testimony, a commenter put this whole chapter in one sentence. God had told him, he wrote, that he couldn't kill something he continued to feed.

This is what Paul means when he tells the Romans to "make no provision for the flesh". "Saturdays only" is a provision. It's a standing reservation: table for one, every Saturday, name on the list. Paul doesn't say to make a smaller provision. He says to make none.

That verse has a history. Augustine was the man who prayed for years, "Give me chastity and continency, only not yet." One day he sat in a garden in Milan, sick of himself. From the house next door he heard a child's voice chanting, "Take up and read." He picked up a book of Paul's letters and read the first lines his eyes fell on. They were this verse: "put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh".

He wrote that he had no wish to read any further, and no need to. "Not yet" was over that afternoon.

![](images/illustrations/cutting-down-augustine-garden.png){.illustration width=80%}

Remember:

**DON'T CUT IT DOWN. CUT IT OUT.**

That's for the last page. Until then you're not on a diet. He bangs, and you're free to go up. Just remember who's the hungry one here, and who's the grown man.

That leaves the smallest request of all: just once. The answer to it comes from a strange place, a book about stalkers.
