# The Counter {#the-counter}

One feature of the Willpower Method makes it worse than everything else, and since the 2010s it has been built into the phone.

If you take a driving test, you know the moment you've passed, because the examiner tells you. On the Willpower Method nobody tells you. The plan is: "If I can just go long enough without it, the wanting will stop." How long is long enough? Nobody can tell you. So you count.

NoFap, the big online movement that began in the 2010s, has made a ritual of this. There are day counters and badges. There is a ninety-day target that its own site describes as "difficult for most, yet achievable." When a man falls, he performs the routine the site calls a reset: "Resetting means restarting their challenge beginning with day one and starting again."

Think about what a counter actually measures. It measures the distance between you and the last time you did it. So every morning it points you back at the habit, the very thing you're trying to leave behind. A man with a streak thinks about porn and masturbation more than a man without one. He has to. He's keeping score.

And who counts days? A man scratching lines on a cell wall counts days. A free man has no idea how many days it's been since he was released. He's got better things to do. Ask a man who used to bite his nails how many days it has been. He couldn't tell you. Each number on that counter says "day forty of doing without", and a man who's doing without has decided in advance that he's losing something.

Then there's the reset. One slip on day sixty-one and the screen says zero. All of it gone! So what does the man at zero do? More often than not he thinks, "Well, it's ruined now," and makes a weekend of it before starting the count again on Monday. The counter turned one bad night into three. A man who had quit for good and stumbled would pick himself up the same hour. The man with a counter has to start again from the bottom of the hill.

So the man on the Willpower Method waits. And while he waits, the questions come:

- "How long is this going to last?"
- "Will I ever enjoy a quiet evening again?"
- "Will I ever sleep properly?"
- "What will I do the next time everything goes wrong?"

He's waiting for things to get better, and while he mopes, the thing he's given up grows more precious by the day. The miserable men on those forums aren't suffering from withdrawal. What hurts them is doubt.

## Meanwhile, on day forty {-}

Back to our man on his streak, because something really is happening to him, though he can't feel it. If he lasts a few weeks, the Little Monster starves. The bodily itch is so slight that he never notices it die.

Around then he feels he's cracked it. So he allows himself one, to prove the point, or to celebrate, or just to see. He does it, and it's a let-down, as it always was. But the Little Monster has had a meal, and next day there's a small voice asking for another. He had been out, and he's walked back in. A week or two later he reasons, "I had one and didn't get hooked, so one more is safe." That's the same reasoning he used at thirteen, and it works the same way now.

Some men do succeed on willpower. I don't want to take anything away from them. But listen to how they tell it. It was long and it was bitter, because the lie was still in their heads the whole time. The Little Monster had been dead for months and they were still moping. At last it dawned on them that they weren't going to give in, the moping stopped, and they found that life went on perfectly well.

Many more fail than succeed that way. And some who succeed stay fragile for the rest of their lives, because a corner of them still believes the habit had something to give. That's why a man can be ten years clear and fall in one bad week. One commenter put all of it into a single line: "I thought I had Kicked the habit but the habit came back."

The most honest account of all this was written two thousand years ago, by a tougher man than you or me. He lost too.
