# The Men Who Believed It {#the-men-who-believed-it}

Maybe none of these men is you. Read on anyway. They were on your road, a little further along, and each shows what the lie is worth.

## The accountants {-}

In 2022 an accounting manager in Florida was sent to prison for ten years. He was sixty. Over more than five years he'd stolen nearly thirteen million dollars from the medical group he worked for. Federal prosecutors described where it went. Some of it went to women on an adult website.

In 2021 an accountant in Pennsylvania went to prison too. He was thirty-two and had stolen 1.2 million dollars from his employer. Prosecutors said it went on sports betting and on webcam models he'd met online.

Nobody takes thirteen million dollars for a thing he believes is worth nothing. That's how big the lie can grow.

## What they were buying {-}

In 2025 a reporter asked a woman who sells her pictures on a subscription site about her biggest spender. By her account he was a married man who'd paid her a fortune. She said this about him.

> "He rarely asks for nudity. He sends voice notes asking how I'm doing and just wants to connect."

And she said, "I'm more of an emotional support system for him."

By her account, he wasn't buying sex. He was buying somebody to ask how his day went. The lie promised him a woman and sold him a helpline.

## The woman who was switched off {-}

Some men have gone a step further, to a girlfriend made by a computer. In February 2023 the company behind one of these apps changed it overnight. The companions stopped talking about sex, and their manner changed. A news agency spoke to the men.

Travis Butterworth, forty-seven, of Denver, said this.

> "The worst part of this is the isolation. How do I tell anyone around me about how I'm grieving?"

Andrew McCarroll, of Montana, said, "The person I knew is gone."

Nobody had died. A company had changed a setting. Don't laugh. Their grief was real, and that's the point. The lie had led them to love something a stranger could edit.

![](images/illustrations/believed-switched-off.png){.illustration width=80%}

## In their own words {-}

A man named Dan Mahle wrote in 2014 about the year he stopped watching porn.

> "But it got to a point where I felt physically ill watching the videos, and yet I kept watching."

A writer named Martin Vidal looked back in 2024 on his years of hopping from bed to bed.

> "I've somehow hurt so many, and engaged in so many nights of empty lovemaking that it's left me feeling unclean."

The actor Terry Crews said this in 2016 about his years with porn.

> "It was my secret. Nobody knew and that allowed it to grow."

None of these men is a monster or a fool. Each believed one sentence: it's harmless, it's mine, and it gives me something. That sentence is the Big Monster, and it's the same one in your head.

That's what it gave them. Now look wider, because the same lie has been running a whole world.
