# The Man Led by a Woman {#the-man-led-by-a-woman}

> "He followed her immediately, as an ox goes to the slaughter"
>
> — Proverbs 7:22

The fourth thing lust borrows is a man's feet. This is the chase. If that has never been you, read this for the man you know who is living it.

There's a man you may recognize at any party. He walks in and goes straight to the women. He doesn't say hello to the men. He buys a round, and every glass goes to a girl. He would tell you he is just a generous, friendly guy.

Try a test. Does he buy the men a drink? Does he spend as much time talking to the men? A generous man is generous with everybody. This man is spending, and he knows what he is shopping for.

The slang of the 2020s calls it simping. The word is new. The problem is very old, and it comes down to one sentence.

**A man ruled by what he wants ends up ruled by whoever can give it to him.**

Think of his wanting as a lever that sticks out of him. Anyone who takes hold of it can move him.

## The man who waits {-}

The man at the bar is the loud kind. There is a quiet kind, and there are far more of him.

He has a friend, she is a woman, and he has been in love with her for three years. He drives her to the airport. He fixes her computer. He listens for an hour while she talks about the man she is actually seeing. He calls it friendship.

But a friend wants nothing back, and this man is keeping count. Every favor is a payment into an account that he believes will pay out one day. She may know it and enjoy the service. She may have no idea. Either way, he isn't being kind to her. He is waiting to be paid. And under the good manners he is angry that the payment hasn't come.

Put the same test to him. Would he do all this for a man? Would he do it for her if he knew for certain that nothing would ever come of it? If the answer is no, it was never a gift.

If that is you, the way out is plain. Stop the favors that are really payments. Tell her once, outright, what you want, or step back and say nothing. Then see what is left. Either the friendship stands on its own, or it never was one.

## Whoever knows what he wants {-}

The lever doesn't care whose hand is on it. A woman can pull it, and so can anyone else who knows what he wants.

The man who makes advertisements knows. That is why a pretty girl is leaning on the car. The stranger who writes to him out of nowhere knows. In her pictures she is young and lovely, and she thinks he is wonderful, and after three weeks she has a trouble that only his money can fix. Very often "she" is a man at a desk, working from a script. Governments know it too. For as long as there have been secrets, the cheapest way to get one has been to send a pretty woman to the man who keeps it.

None of these people is stronger or cleverer than he is. They don't need to be. They only need to know where the lever is.

## The strongest man in the Bible {-}

None of this is new. The oldest full account of it is in the Bible, in the book of Judges, and it is about a man named Samson. He was the strongest man in the Bible. God gave him that strength to free his people from their enemies, the Philistines, and no army could hold him. His weakness was women, and his enemies knew it. He fell in love with a woman named Delilah, and the Philistine rulers came to her with an offer. "Entice him, and see in which his great strength lies." Each of them promised her eleven hundred pieces of silver.

Three times she asked him for his secret. Three times he told her a lie, and each time she tried it on him with armed men hiding in the next room.

Stop there and look at him. Three times this woman has tried to hand him to men who want him dead. He knows it. And he stays. A man with his wits about him takes the hint after one ambush. That is what lust does to a strong man's judgment. He isn't fooled. He just can't leave.

Then she used the oldest line there is. "How can you say, 'I love you,' when your heart is not with me?" She "pressed him daily with her words" until he was worn out, and at last he told her the truth. His strength was tied to a vow of dedication to God, and its sign was that his hair had never been cut. She put him to sleep on her knees, called a man to shave his head, and collected her silver.

He woke up sure that he would shake himself free as before. "But he didn't know that the LORD had departed from him." The Philistines put out his eyes, the eyes that had led him all his life, "and he ground at the mill in the prison".

That is not quite the end. His hair grew again, and on his last day, blind and brought out to be laughed at, he prayed, and God heard him. A ruined man can still be heard. But he never got back his sight, or the wasted years.

Notice where the story puts the blame. Delilah sold him for silver, and that was treachery. But nobody took his secret from him. He gave it away.

Samson at least was fooled by a woman he loved. The next man wasn't fooled at all.
