# In Bed with the Phone {#in-bed-with-the-phone}

If you're like most men, the phone is in your hand before your head has touched the pillow. What follows starts without any decision at all. Try to remember each time you did it last week. You'll recall one or two, the most recent or the one you'd waited for, and the others have vanished. That's how little they meant.

"I do it when I'm bored." But boredom is a state of mind. Give a man something that absorbs him and he can go for days without a thought of it. Leave him lying on a bed with nothing to do and a screen full of half-dressed strangers. Now there's nothing between him and the Little Monster's whisper. He isn't bored and then tempted. He's itchy, and with nothing to distract him he notices it.

The same psychiatrist says the number one cause of relapse is not strong emotion. It is boredom. His reason takes one line: "if you've got a gap in your day porn will fill it".

The most famous fall in the Bible began in an empty hour. David, Israel's greatest king, fell on an idle evening, alone on his roof, while his army was away at war. You know that hour.

And far from curing boredom, the habit breeds it. Afterward you're heavy and dull. You don't get up and call a friend or fix the shelf. You lie there and scroll. Boredom is what tells a man that he ought to be doing something, and the habit switches it off.

## "It clears my head" {-}

This is the opposite claim: now the habit is supposed to help you concentrate. You sit down to the essay or the tax return and you're stuck. The itch nags, so you "get it out of the way", go back to the desk, and conclude that it helped.

It didn't.

It removed a distraction that it had planted, and it sent you back with a duller head. The man who never had the habit got stuck, stayed in his chair and got unstuck.

## The shower and the quiet evening {-}

"It relaxes me."

Does it? There's a locked door, and one ear is listening for footsteps on the stairs. You delete the history and cover your tracks. In the shower you keep an eye on the clock, because people are waiting for the bathroom. A man who is relaxing doesn't behave like that. He looks like a burglar.

Take the morning shower. It's the habit at its most mechanical: warm water, a locked door, five minutes, the same as yesterday. Nothing in you asked for it. The shower asked for it.

Now take the evening. A man comes in from work, eats, puts his feet up, and he's content. The man with the habit can't quite be. He has another appetite waiting, and until it's fed he's not fully in the room. He'll hurry the children's bedtime story. He'll be short with his wife for no reason she can find. He'll wait for the house to go to bed. Afterward, for half an hour, he's as calm as other men are all evening.

Men call it the perfect end to the day. It's a debt collector at the door.

Why is a free man relaxed without it? And why can't you relax after one or two days without? Read what men write in the first week of a willpower "streak": pacing, snapping at people, counting the hours. That's what a man looks like when the single comfort he thinks he's entitled to has been taken away. He has forgotten what ordinary peace feels like.

## "I can't sleep without it" {-}

For many men this is the last one to fall, because the fear feels so practical. "I've got work in the morning. I can't lie awake."

So ask the plain questions. How did you sleep when you were eight? How do the men who've stopped sleep? How did you sleep in a tent, in a barracks, in your in-laws' guest room?

You've turned the last cue of the day into a sleeping pill. The bed is the cue. Lights out is the cue. So every night the Little Monster wakes up just as you lie down, and you can't settle until he's been fed. Then you sleep, and you thank the habit. It was the habit that kept you awake.

It's a poor sedative in any case. The session runs late and the phone runs later, and at seven the next morning a man drags himself out of bed and blames the mattress.

When you stop, you may have two or three nights when the old cue fires and you lie there for a while. Don't dread them. Lie there and smile, because you know what's dying. After that you'll sleep the way you did as a boy. David wrote of it in Psalm 4: "In peace I will both lay myself down and sleep, for you, LORD alone, make me live in safety." He says he'll sleep in peace. He doesn't say he'll sleep worn out.

## Energy {-}

Most men know the flat ten minutes afterward. Fewer notice what the habit does to their energy across years, because it happens so slowly.

The tiredness gets filed under "getting older". Your friends are tired too, so it seems normal. At twenty-eight you talk like an old man, and you assume that drive belongs to teenagers.

Suppose I could lift you out of today and set you down three weeks after stopping. It would be just for a morning, so that you felt the difference all at once. That alone would do it. You wouldn't say, "Is this how good it gets?" You'd say, "Had I really sunk that far?" I can't carry you there. You can, though.

I can't prove this with a lab report. This is testimony, but it is remarkably consistent. One man wrote, thirty days in:

> "First off, I finally have energy again! I haven't felt this good since high school."

The energy comes back fast. It starts within days, on one condition. If you stop by willpower, feeling deprived, the gloom will swallow every gain. Stop this book's way and you get to enjoy them from the first morning.

One occasion is left. It lies underneath all the others, and it is the saddest.
