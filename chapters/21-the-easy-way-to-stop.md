# The Easy Way to Stop

> "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and righteous to forgive us the sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness."
>
> — 1 John 1:9

You know the trap, the lies, and every wrong way out. This is the right one.

Follow these instructions and you'll find stopping somewhere between easy and enjoyable. There are only two:

1. **Decide that you are finished with lust in all five ways: look, think, touch, chase, take. Never again.**
2. **Don't mope about it. Rejoice.**

The Bible has older names for both. The first is *repentance*. The word doesn't mean feeling terrible. It means a change of mind: you turn around and walk the other way. The second is *receiving grace*. The believer is forgiven, so he acts like a forgiven man.

Decide and rejoice, or repent and receive. That's the whole method. A Christian calls both halves gifts from God.

This book has asked no vow of you, and for good reason. The old vow was a promise to go without. This decision is only a refusal to go back for nothing. It comes at the end, when you no longer want what you're leaving, and that's why it holds.

"Then why all these chapters? Why didn't you say so on page one?"

Because you've done the first half before. You decided, with tears, more times than you can count. Then you moped, the moping turned to craving, and one gray evening you un-decided. The decisions didn't stick because you still believed you were giving something up. That belief is the Big Monster, and it had to come down lie by lie before these two lines could work. Once you see how the trap is built, walking out is simple.

## One man's last time {-}

Remember the man on the couch, with his phone face down on the cushion? Here's how it ends for him. He's spent years on the other method: vows at retreats, on New Year's Day, on mornings when he couldn't look at himself. Then one day a new thought scares him for real. *What if I just do this for the rest of my life?* For once he stops vowing and starts watching. What is he actually getting? A few seconds of relief, then the same old flatness.

He talks to men who've quit, expecting to find them barely hanging on. Most of them laugh. "Miss it? You're joking." So he says to himself, out loud: "Like it or not, that was the last time." He braces for months of misery. What he gets is relief on day one, and it doesn't leave.

Why so smooth? The terrible cravings he braced for don't exist. Doubt makes them. A man suffers only when he wants the habit and thinks he can't have it. Even in the trap you go whole days without it when you're busy or traveling, and you barely notice. The misery comes from wavering: the "maybe just tonight", the door left open a crack.

So when your turn comes, make the decision certain and final. Don't hope you've stopped.

Know it.

## Free from the first day {-}

The moment you decide, you're already a man who doesn't do it. Not after ninety days. Not when the Little Monster dies. In that moment. A Christian will say that's because of what Christ has already done. A recovery writer put the new identity in a single line: "Begin to say, I am a man (or woman) who does not use pornography, masturbate, or lust."

Don't wait for the itch to fade before you let yourself be happy. Get out and live.

The Christian has something more, if he'll take it. The apostle John wrote, "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and righteous to forgive us the sins". Confess, and he is *faithful* to forgive. It doesn't say he might, or that he will once you've racked up a decent streak. And in Romans 8:1: "There is therefore now no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus." The word is *now*, on day one, with the Little Monster still grumbling.

One man, who now teaches other men about this, spent years repenting and falling. Then he saw what was wrong with his repentance. It was all about him: how bad he was, how hard he'd try. "The foundation of my repentance was me", he says. Then he turned it around, and this is the sentence to carry out of this chapter: "Our motive for repentance is the fact that God is good. Not the fact that you are bad."

Let your sorrow look up, not in. Be sorry for what this has cost the people you love. Then get up.

Guilt loads the gun. The man who decides and then spends three weeks feeling filthy is moping in religious clothes, and he ends up in the same place. Grace is what makes instruction two possible. You rejoice because the past really is dealt with. A man of forty-three, who'd fasted and read his Bible over it for years, summed up his freedom in three words and capital letters: "JESUS DID IT!" That's what rejoicing sounds like, and you can start on day one.

Dr. Kanojia, the psychiatrist, has noticed what frightens his patients about stopping: "if I stop, I have to face the guilt of having screwed up the last 10 years of my life." That fear has an answer. The ten years are confessed and forgiven. You don't have to carry them, and you don't have to keep the habit going to avoid looking at them.

If you're not a believer, the same advice holds: drop the self-hatred. It never helped you once. The years are spent either way, and hating yourself buys none of them back. What's still yours is the next ten. Decide, and be glad.

## A man who can be trusted {-}

This book said one hard thing early on: **a man who can't control his lust can't be trusted.** Now the other half.

You probably thought self-control meant gripping the rail, a strong man holding down a strong desire. That's a wrestling match, and you know who usually won.

Real self-control is quieter. It belongs to a man whose mind is no longer divided. He isn't holding anything down, because nothing's pushing up. Thomas Chalmers, a Scottish preacher of two hundred years ago, explained how a desire leaves a heart: "the only way to dispossess it of an old affection is by the expulsive power of a new one." For Chalmers the new affection was love for God himself, and he held that nothing smaller is strong enough. If you're not a believer, take the half that holds for any man. A want is never pushed out by force. It goes when you see it never gave you anything.

The payoff comes at once. The moment you decide and mean it, you've given your word and you're going to keep it. For the first time in years you can believe your own promises. Trust starts there, and your wife, your friends and your work will feel it in time.

## Two warnings {-}

**First: don't make the decision tonight.** It has its own place, a few chapters on. A decision made on the strength of one good chapter is how the old vows were made. If you've already stopped, stay stopped, and keep reading. The chapters between here and there are what keep a man from sliding back.

**Second: don't misread "the first weeks".** The Little Monster takes a few weeks to starve. That doesn't mean a few weeks of suffering. And it doesn't mean holding out until day twenty-one, because nothing magic happens on day twenty-one. A man who mopes for three weeks will be moping in the fourth.

You can't order yourself to feel glad about losing something you still value. But you aren't losing anything. Once you know that, the gladness comes by itself.

So what will the first weeks actually feel like?
