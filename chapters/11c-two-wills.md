# Two Wills {#two-wills}

If you want the Willpower Method described by someone who wasn't selling an app, read Paul. In Romans 7 he writes:

"For the good which I desire, I don't do; but the evil which I don't desire, that I practice."

No man has written a more honest sentence about himself. And Paul was no weakling. He was beaten, shipwrecked and pelted with stones, and he kept going. If grit were the cure, he had more of it than you or I ever will. Yet when his own will went up against his own flesh, he lost, and he said so.

There are two wills in you. One wants to be free, and the other wants one more night. The Willpower Method sets the first to overpower the second, every day, for good. But it's all one man. You're arm-wrestling yourself, and whichever arm wins, you're the one who's tired. One commenter wrote a line that thousands of other men liked: "Every time I think I've escaped this sin, it comes back at me like a bite of a snake."

These men aren't lazy and they aren't faithless. They tried as hard as anyone could, and trying harder was the one thing that couldn't work.

Now look at how Paul ends the chapter. "What a wretched man I am! Who will deliver me out of the body of this death? I thank God through Jesus Christ, our Lord!"

His question isn't "*how* shall I try harder?" It's "*who* will deliver me?" The answer is a person and a gift, and it comes with a shout of thanks. He never mentions a technique.

If you're not a believer, part of this holds without the theology. The war between two wills doesn't end when one of them finally wins. **IT ENDS WHEN ONE OF THEM IS GONE.** The will that wants "one more night" lives entirely on the belief that the night has something to offer. Take away the belief and there's no second will left to fight. That's what this book is doing, a chapter at a time. For a believer, that is the work Paul gave thanks for. The one who delivers does it, and this book only clears the ground.

## Enjoyment never came into it {-}

Enjoyment has nothing to do with the habit, and it never did. We only assume we enjoy it because the alternative is too insulting to consider. The alternative is twenty years spent on something that gives us nothing. That's why most of it happens half-asleep, on autopilot, in the dark. A man at this habit is only content while he isn't thinking about what he's doing.

The Willpower Method gets self-control wrong. It thinks self-control means holding a door shut for life, with something heavy leaning on the other side. That isn't self-control. It's a siege, and sooner or later the man inside gets tired. A soldier who spends every watch wrestling the bottle isn't fit for duty.

He is only dry.

Real self-control looks different. There's nothing leaning on the door. The man opens it and shuts it as he pleases, and half the time he forgets it's there.

The old Christians who say a man must keep watch his whole life are right. Peter, one of the twelve men closest to Jesus, said, "Be sober and self-controlled. Be watchful." But a watchman doesn't wrestle the door every night. He knows what's out there, and he isn't afraid of it.

The habit feeds the Little Monster. The lies are the Big Monster. Starve the first, clear out the second, and you will have **NEITHER THE NEED NOR THE WISH** to go back to any of it.

There is one more way men try to do it by willpower. It sounds like the sensible one, and it is the worst.
