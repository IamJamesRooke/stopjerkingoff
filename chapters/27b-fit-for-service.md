# Fit for Service {#fit-for-service}

> "No soldier on duty entangles himself in the affairs of life, that he may please him who enrolled him as a soldier."
>
> — 2 Timothy 2:4

You're not only being set free from something. You're being set free for something.

## Who gains? {-}

Ask one more question. Who gains from your habit?

You don't. So who does? There are companies that earn more the longer you sit aroused, alone and slumped on the couch. And behind the companies, something thrives when men are numb and spent. A Christian has a name for that something, and Peter warned the people he loved about him: "Be sober and self-controlled. Be watchful. Your adversary, the devil, walks around like a roaring lion, seeking whom he may devour." If you don't believe in a devil, call it a business model. It's working on you either way.

And it never has to fight you. Take a man with people to love and work to do. Maybe he has a house to frame, a school to start, a song to finish. How do you stop him? Show him a little skin on a screen and he walks himself off the field. Twenty minutes later he's spent, slumped in the blue glow, and no use to anybody until morning. Then you do it again the next night. It's the cheapest victory there is. The enemy of mankind has an old trick, and it still works on a boy of fourteen.

None of that is an excuse. The apostle James won't let a man say the devil made him do it: "each one is tempted when he is drawn away by his own lust and enticed." The bait dangles, but the appetite is yours. That's good news, because it means the way out is yours as well.

Think what that says about you. Nobody bothers to distract a man who doesn't matter. You were worth taking out of the fight.

It worked on a king. David took another man's wife and had her husband Uriah killed to hide it. Uriah slept at the palace door, drunk, and was still fit for service. David, sober, was no use to anybody.

## The deal he turned down {-}

The oldest offer on the table was made to Jesus himself. Luke tells it. Jesus went out into the wilderness for forty days and ate nothing. At the end of it he was starving and alone, as weak as a man gets. That's when the devil showed up.

He took Jesus up high and showed him all the kingdoms of the world in a single moment. All of it had been handed over to him, the devil said, and he could give it to anyone he liked. "If you therefore will worship before me, it will all be yours."

Jesus said, "Get behind me, Satan!"

Notice one thing he didn't say. He never said, "That isn't yours to give." He turned the deal down flat, and he didn't argue with the claim.

So picture the world as the devil's shop, for now. Every voice in it telling you to keep feeding the Little Monster comes from someone who took the deal Jesus refused: a kingdom, a market, a crowd, in exchange for a soul.

If you don't believe in a devil, the story still lands. Every empire of easy pleasure was built by somebody who said yes to that offer, and you're the one paying for his kingdom.

## A living sacrifice {-}

Plenty of faiths honor the man who dies for what he believes, and Christianity is one of them. The church has its martyrs, and it's right to honor them. But Paul asks every believer for something that sounds smaller and is harder. "Present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable to God." Not only a death. A life, one ordinary Tuesday after another.

Jesus showed both. He hung on the cross for about six hours, from the third hour of the day to the ninth. Once he was nailed there, there was no tapping out. But before the cross came the days nobody paints: forty days hungry in the desert, and some thirty years of plain work and plain days before that.

Paul says Jesus didn't cling to his rights as God. He took the form of a servant and was made like us. He stayed who he was, and he took on our limits: hunger, tiredness, temptation, all of it. Then he walked through them and showed how it's done.

That's what's asked of you. Not one heroic night. The next ordinary evening, and the one after it, lived on purpose.

If you're not a believer, you know this already. Any man can be brave for one loud minute. The man people lean on is the one who shows up every day.

And for this one thing, the daily sacrifice costs less than you'd think. Nothing precious goes on the altar. You're just turning down a pest's dinner order.

## Drunk on duty {-}

You may be a Christian, on God's side and loved by him. "There is therefore now no condemnation" is as true today as it ever was. And you were made on purpose. Paul says the work was planned as well as the man: "we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared before that we would walk in them." There's something with your name on it that nobody else is going to do.

But a man ruled by lust is still the soldier who shows up drunk for duty. He's loved, he's enlisted, and he's unfit for service. He can't fight at full strength, and the people leaning on him get a fraction of him. You know who they are. Paul puts being sober and being armed in one breath: "since we belong to the day, let's be sober, putting on the breastplate of faith and love, and for a helmet, the hope of salvation." Sober comes first, and then the armor.

So get the order right. Men who have quit drinking have a saying that fits here: whatever you put ahead of staying sober, you'll lose. Read "sober" as "free of lust". For a Christian, God comes first, and getting free is how a man gets back to putting him there. Putting it first doesn't mean building your life around it. It means settling it, so you can get on with everything else. A sober soldier doesn't spend the day thinking about drink. He does his job.

If you're not a believer, keep the picture and change the flag. You still have people who depend on you and work that's yours to do. You've been showing up to both with half your mind in the back room. The order's the same for you.

Here's the good news, and the reason this book exists. Lust isn't hard to settle. Nobody's sending you on a ten-year campaign. You've read this far, and you know what lust is: a trick, an itch that feeds itself, a lie about comfort. Putting down a thing you've seen through takes no courage. Save yours for the service. You'll need it there. The first job will be small: one man who's still where you were.

And being fit isn't the price of being loved. It's what a loved man is for.

The prophet Isaiah once saw a vision of God on his throne. His first words were, "Woe is me!", because he knew what he was. Then an angel touched his lips with a coal from the altar and told him his sin was forgiven. And when God asked, "Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?", the forgiven man didn't hesitate. He said, "Here I am. Send me!"
