# Research dossier: "Marriage is a scam rigged against men"

Compiled 22 November 2026. United States unless marked otherwise.

## How to read this

- **PRIMARY** = I opened the paper, statute, court opinion or government report itself. **SECONDARY** = I opened a news story, press release or summary of it.
- **Important limit on every quote.** Pages were read through a fetch tool that passes the page to a small extraction model and returns its answer. That model refuses to return any quotation longer than about 125 characters, and direct downloads were blocked by the network policy. So: (a) every quote below is short; (b) where it returned only part of a sentence I label it FRAGMENT and it must not be printed as a full sentence or joined to another piece; (c) each quote should be checked by eye against the URL before it goes to print. Figures were returned the same way and deserve the same check.
- Pages that would not open: PubMed Central (captcha), Springer, SAGE, Stateline's own site. Where that forced me onto a mirror or a summary, the item is marked.
- "Does NOT show" is given for each item.

## 1. Who files for divorce

**1a. Wives want the divorce in about two thirds of cases; in unmarried breakups there is no gap.** PRIMARY (author's paper on his Stanford page).
- Figures: 69% of marital breakups were wanted more by the wife (95% CI 61-78%), from 92 divorces. Cohabiting unmarried couples: 56% wanted by the woman (CI 47-65%, 76 breakups), not distinguishable from 50%. Dating, never cohabited: 53.4% (CI 47.9-58.9%, 203 breakups). Mutual breakups: 19% of divorces, 32-35% of unmarried breakups.
- Married women reported lower relationship quality than married men (4.46 v 4.61 on a 5-point scale); among cohabiters the gap was small and not significant.
- Source: Rosenfeld, "Who wants the Breakup? Gender and Breakup in Heterosexual Couples", How Couples Meet and Stay Together survey, 2009-2015, 2,538 adults at start. https://web.stanford.edu/~mrosenfe/Rosenfeld_gender_of_breakup.pdf
- Verbatim (abstract): "The results show that only in marriages are the majority of breakups wanted by the female partner."
- Does NOT show: why. Only 92 divorces. It measures who *wanted* the breakup as reported by one partner, not who filed. It does not show wives leave for money: if a legal payout drove it, one would still need to explain why married women also report lower satisfaction. Rosenfeld's own test of whether women are simply more sensitive to relationship quality was not supported.

**1b. Court records agree: wives file about two thirds, and expected custody is the strongest predictor of who files.** PRIMARY (paper PDF; last 4% of text not read).
- Figures: 1995 divorce certificates from Connecticut, Virginia, Oregon, Montana, n = 46,547. Wives filed slightly over two thirds (Connecticut 69.4%; 71.3% where there were children). About 60% through most of the 19th century, so this long predates no-fault. Cruelty grounds were only about 6% of Virginia divorces.
- Source: Brinig and Allen, "These Boots Are Made for Walking: Why Most Divorce Filers Are Women", American Law and Economics Review, 2000. https://econ586.turchi.sites.oasis.unc.edu/Readings/Brinig.pdf
- FRAGMENT (do not print as a sentence): "who gets the children is by far the most important component in deciding who files for divorce"
- This is the best scholarly support for the skeptic's "incentive" point: the spouse who expects to keep the children is the one who files. CONTESTED in interpretation: the authors frame it as rational self-interest; Rosenfeld frames the gap as dissatisfaction with marriage as an institution. Both can be true.
- Does NOT show: that women file to profit. It is 1995 data from four states. Filing is not the same as causing the breakdown.

**1c. Older survey: two thirds of midlife divorces asked for by the wife; reasons differ by sex.** SECONDARY (AP report, 27 May 2004, on CBS News).
- Figures: AARP survey of 1,147 people aged 40-79 who divorced in their 40s, 50s or 60s; margin of error 3 points. Women asked for the divorce in about two thirds of cases. Women's top reasons: physical or emotional abuse, infidelity, drug or alcohol abuse. Men's top reasons: falling out of love, different values or lifestyles, infidelity. 26% of men said they "never saw it coming" against 14% of women.
- https://www.cbsnews.com/news/wives-taking-point-seeking-divorce
- Does NOT show: anything about people under 40; reasons are self-reported and one-sided. I did not open the AARP report itself.

## 2. Custody

**2a. Four in five custodial parents are mothers.** PRIMARY (Census report P60-269).
- Figures, 2018: 12.9 million custodial parents; 79.9% mothers, 20.1% fathers. The father share was 16.0% in 1994.
- Verbatim: "about 4 of every 5 (79.9 percent) of the 12.9 million custodial parents were mothers." (FRAGMENT of a longer sentence.)
- https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2020/demo/p60-269.pdf
- Does NOT show: court bias. This counts all custodial parents, including never-married mothers where no court ever ruled, and it counts whoever the child lives with most; 50/50 arrangements are poorly captured.

**2b. Almost no custody outcomes are decided by a judge.** PRIMARY (Mnookin's own article; figures are from his footnote 71).
- Figures: Stanford/California study of divorcing families (mid-1980s filings): of 933 cases, a judge decided 14.
- Verbatim: "Of the 933 cases in our study, a judge decided only fourteen, which is 1.5%."
- Source: Robert Mnookin, "Child Custody Revisited", 77 Law and Contemporary Problems 249 (2014). https://core.ac.uk/download/28950737.pdf
- Does NOT show: that the settlements were free choices. Parents bargain "in the shadow of the law": a father who expects to lose may not ask. One California sample, two counties, forty years old. The article did not give what mothers and fathers each requested (that is in the 1992 book *Dividing the Child*, which I did not open).

**2c. Shared custody has grown sharply.** PRIMARY (University of Wisconsin Institute for Research on Poverty report, 2020, citing Meyer, Cancian and Cook 2017).
- Figures, Wisconsin divorce court records: shared placement was 12% of cases in 1989 (5% equal, 7% unequal) and 50% in 2010 (15% equal, 35% unequal). Mother-sole placement fell; father-sole stayed about the same.
- https://www.irp.wisc.edu/wp/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/CS-2018-2020-Task-12.pdf
- Does NOT show: the national picture. Wisconsin is ahead of most states; the report's own national survey data are thin (23 Wisconsin cases in the latest cohort).

**2d. Several states now presume equal parenting time.** SECONDARY (Stateline article, September 2026, read on the Missouri Independent mirror; Stateline's own page would not open).
- States named: Kentucky (2018), Arkansas (2021), West Virginia (2022), Florida (2023), Missouri (2023), and Louisiana and Mississippi ("this year", i.e. 2026 per the article).
- https://missouriindependent.com/2026/09/18/repub/more-states-embrace-50-50-child-custody-in-divorce-cases/
- Does NOT show: that these laws work. The article reports Kentucky's divorce rate fell 25% from 2016 to 2023 against 18% nationally, but names no source and a quoted demographer says there is no single cause. A presumption is rebuttable and applies mainly to cases that reach a judge. I did not open the statutes.

**2e. When judges are tested in an experiment, they do favour mothers a little.** SECONDARY (University of Illinois news release, 19 April 2018, on Andrea Miller's study "Expertise fails to attenuate gendered biases in judicial decision-making").
- Figures: 619 sitting trial judges and 504 laypeople in one unnamed state read identical cases with only the parent's sex changed; 372 judges ruled on the custody case. Judges gave the mother about half a day more parenting time per week; laypeople 0.15 of a day.
- https://news.illinois.edu/study-judges-as-susceptible-to-gender-bias-as-laypeople-and-sometimes-more-so/
- Does NOT show: real rulings. A paper vignette, one state. The measured bias is real but modest: half a day a week, not "fathers get nothing".

**2f. What happens when fathers actually contest. CONTESTED.**
- The claim often repeated: fathers who actively seek custody get primary or joint physical custody "over 70% of the time". Origin: Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court Gender Bias Study (published 1990). SECONDARY: I read an excerpt on a blog-hosted copy (https://amptoons.com/blog/files/Massachusetts_Gender_Bias_Study.htm); the excerpt gave no sample size or years.
- The other side: fathers' advocates answer that this is selection. Only fathers with unusually strong cases (already the main carer, or an unfit mother) fight, so their success rate says nothing about the average father. Brinig and Allen (1b) and Mnookin (2b) both support the view that expectations shape who asks.
- Safe wording: "Mothers end up with the children far more often, but mostly because parents agree to it, and mothers did more of the child care before the split. Where bias has been measured directly it exists and is small. Nobody has good national data on what happens when an ordinary father asks for half."
- Background on care before divorce (SECONDARY, Bureau of Labor Statistics figures as cited by Stateline): mothers of children under 6 spend about 7.5 hours a day with them, fathers a little over 5.

## 3. Child support

**3a. Nine in ten payers are fathers; the typical amount is modest.** PRIMARY (Census, "Support Providers: 2013", P70BR-158, published 2018).
- Figures, 2013: 5.8 million parents paid child support; 90.6% fathers. Median paid by fathers $4,765 a year; mean $6,312 (about 10.7% of family income).
- https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2018/demo/P70BR-158.pdf
- Does NOT show: the burden on high earners or in high-cost states, where orders run far above the median.

**3b. Less than half of parents owed support get all of it.** PRIMARY (Census P60-269).
- Figures, 2017: 45.9% received full payment, 24.0% part, 30.2% none. Mean due $5,519; mean received $3,431; median received $1,800. $30.0 billion due, $18.6 billion (62.2%) received. Only 49.4% of custodial parents had any order or agreement (2018). Poverty: custodial mothers 27.3%, custodial fathers 11.2%.
- URL as 2a.
- Worth noting for fairness: custodial fathers owed support were *less* likely to be paid (38.4% got nothing, against 28.7% of mothers).
- Does NOT show: why payers do not pay (cannot or will not).

**3c. Most of the debt is owed by men with almost no income.** PRIMARY (HHS/ASPE executive summary, 2007, of the Urban Institute study of nine large states).
- Figures: national arrears $105.4 billion (September 2006). In the nine states, 70% of arrears were owed by obligors with no reported income or income of $10,000 a year or less (2003/04 data); 11% of debtors owed 54% of the debt. Routine charging of interest was the main reason debt grew.
- https://aspe.hhs.gov/execsum/assessing-child-support-arrears-nine-large-states-and-nation
- Does NOT show: today's totals. Data are twenty years old; "no reported income" includes men working off the books.

**3d. Men are jailed for non-payment, and the Supreme Court found one such jailing unlawful.** PRIMARY (opinion, Turner v. Rogers, 564 U.S. 431, decided 20 June 2011).
- Facts: a South Carolina father ordered to pay $51.73 a week, $5,728.76 behind, was given 12 months for civil contempt at a hearing with no lawyer and no finding that he could pay; he served it all. The Court held there is no automatic right to a lawyer, but his jailing violated due process because the court never determined his ability to pay.
- https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/10-10
- How often it happens: unknown. PRIMARY (Congressional Research Service R42389, 6 March 2012): "National data do not exist with respect to how often the incarceration option is used." The same report says probably under 2% of collections can be tied to the threat of jail, and that all 50 states have criminal non-support statutes. https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20120306_R42389_6ed20f2c604a40a4124ef74c96de1e992b29e090.html
- Does NOT show: any reliable count of men jailed.

**3e. Paternity: a man can be held to support a child who is not his.** PRIMARY (HHS/ASPE symposium summary, published 2007).
- Facts: a husband is presumed the legal father of a child born in the marriage. An unmarried man who signs a voluntary acknowledgment has 60 days to rescind; after that it can be challenged only for fraud, duress or material mistake of fact, and state rules on reopening vary widely. Los Angeles County reported about 70% of its orders were entered by default.
- The "30% of paternity tests show he is not the father" figure: the report traces it to blood-bank laboratory data (about 354,000 tests in 2003) and says there is no evidence it applies to the general population, since tests are ordered when paternity is already doubted and several men may be tested for one child. In undisputed samples the cited range was 1.4% to 18%.
- https://www.aspe.hhs.gov/reports/emerging-issues-paternity-establishment-symposium-summary
- Does NOT show: how many men are in this position. No one has counted.

## 4. Alimony

**4a. Alimony is rare.** SECONDARY (Associated Press, 22 December 2017, on PBS NewsHour). This is the best figure I found and it is weak.
- Figures: IRS, 2015: 361,000 taxpayers claimed they paid alimony, totalling $9.6 billion; 178,000 reported receiving it. Census Bureau: 243,000 people received alimony "last year"; 98% of them women.
- https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/how-the-tax-overhaul-will-affect-alimony-deductions
- A second count, SECONDARY (Tax Foundation, 23 May 2014, reporting a Treasury Inspector General audit): 567,887 returns claimed the deduction for tax year 2010, over $10 billion. https://taxfoundation.org/blog/treasury-finds-23-billion-discrepancies-alimony-deduction/
- Weaknesses: the three counts disagree (178,000 to 568,000); I did not open the Census table or the audit; survey respondents under-report; since 2019 alimony under new decrees is no longer deductible, so the tax trail has gone. Against roughly 13 million custodial parents and tens of millions of divorced adults, any of these numbers means only a small share of divorced men pay it.
- Does NOT show: the share of *divorces* with an alimony award. I found no trustworthy modern figure for that (see DO NOT PRINT).
- Fair point for the skeptic: where it is paid, it is paid almost entirely by men, and the average implied by the IRS figures is large (about $26,000 a year per payer).

**4b. Florida ended permanent alimony in 2023.** PRIMARY (Florida Senate bill summary, CS/SB 1416) plus SECONDARY (CBS Miami, 30 June 2023, for the signing date).
- Facts: permanent alimony eliminated; no durational alimony for marriages under 3 years; rehabilitative alimony capped at 5 years; the award limited to the lesser of reasonable need or 35% of the difference in the parties' net incomes; effective 1 July 2023; passed 34-6 and 102-12; signed 30 June 2023 after three earlier vetoes of similar bills.
- https://flsenate.gov/Committees/BillSummaries/2023/html/1416 and https://www.cbsnews.com/miami/news/florida-gov-desantis-signs-bill-ending-permanent-alimony
- Does NOT show: the position in other states. Rules vary greatly.

## 5. Money, health and children after divorce

**5a. The famous "women fall 73%, men rise 42%" figure was wrong.** SECONDARY (Wilson Quarterly, "Farewell to a Factoid"; I did not open Peterson's paper itself).
- Figures: Weitzman's 1985 book claimed a 73% fall for women and a 42% rise for men. Richard Peterson re-analysed her own data and found a 27% fall for women and a 10% rise for men (American Sociological Review, June 1996). Weitzman accepted the original number was wrong.
- https://www.wilsonquarterly.com/quarterly/_/farewell-factoid/
- Does NOT show: today's picture. Los Angeles divorces from 1977. "Standard of living" is income adjusted for household size, so a man who moves out alone scores as richer even if he feels poorer.

**5b. Newer work: men hurt more at first, women lose more money for longer. GERMANY, not the US.** SECONDARY (University of Amsterdam repository abstract page; publisher's and PMC full text would not open).
- Leopold, "Gender Differences in the Consequences of Divorce: A Study of Multiple Outcomes", Demography, 2018. German Socio-Economic Panel 1984-2015, 18,030 people, 1,220 divorces, 20 outcomes.
- Findings: men were hit harder in the short term on well-being; in the medium term the sexes looked alike on health, mental health and loneliness; the lasting difference was women's larger loss of household income, with more poverty and single parenting.
- FRAGMENTS only (do not join): "men's disproportionate strain of divorce is transient" / "women's is chronic".
- https://dare.uva.nl/id/a46e7b43-2070-45b2-9b81-447f753b91f9
- Does NOT show: the United States. I could not retrieve the exact income percentages.

**5c. Current US snapshot: divorced people of both sexes are poorer; divorced men work less.** PRIMARY (Pew Research Center, "8 facts about divorce in the United States", 16 October 2025, using federal survey data).
- Figures (working-age adults): median household wealth: divorced $98,700; first marriage $326,900; never married $32,600. Median household income: divorced $84,900; first marriage $118,600. Employment: divorced men 73% against 88% of men in a first marriage; divorced women 76% against 71%. Pew notes research finds unemployed married men are more likely to divorce, so the arrow may run the other way.
- https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/10/16/8-facts-about-divorce-in-the-united-states.md
- Does NOT show: that divorce caused the gap, or any split of the money figures by sex.

**5d. Divorced men's suicide risk is more than double married men's.** PRIMARY (journal page, abstract and text).
- Figures: Kposowa, Journal of Epidemiology and Community Health, 2000. National Longitudinal Mortality Study, 1979-1989. Divorced or separated people: relative risk 2.08 (CI 1.58-2.72). Divorced men against married men: 2.38 (CI 1.77-3.20). Among women, no significant difference by marital status.
- Verbatim: "Marital status, especially divorce, has strong net effect on mortality from suicide, but only among men."
- https://jech.bmj.com/content/54/4/254.full
- Does NOT show: cause. Men who are depressed or drinking are more likely both to be divorced and to die by suicide. Data are from the 1980s.

**5e. Many fathers who live apart from their children see them rarely.** PRIMARY (Pew, "A Tale of Two Fathers", 2011, using the federal National Survey of Family Growth 2006-08).
- Figures: of fathers living apart from minor children, about one in five saw them more than once a week; 29% at least monthly; 21% several times a year; 27% had no visit in the past year.
- https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2011/06/15/a-tale-of-two-fathers/markdown
- Does NOT show: divorced fathers specifically (never-married fathers are included and see children less), nor whether the father was kept away or stayed away.

## 6. No-fault divorce

**6a. What and when.** SECONDARY (ABA Journal, 1 November 2010).
- No-fault divorce lets one spouse end a marriage without proving the other did wrong (adultery, cruelty, desertion). California's law was signed in 1969; Florida followed in 1971; New York was the last state, in 2010.
- https://www.abajournal.com/magazine/article/and_then_there_was_none
- The usual statement that California's law took effect on 1 January 1970 is correct to my knowledge but the page I opened gives only the 1969 signing. Print "signed in 1969" or check the effective date separately.

**6b. It raised divorce for about ten years, then the effect faded.** PRIMARY (NBER working paper page; abstract returned in fragments).
- Wolfers, "Did Unilateral Divorce Laws Raise Divorce Rates?", NBER 10014, 2003 (later American Economic Review, 2006).
- FRAGMENTS: "liberalized divorce laws caused a discernible rise in divorce rates for about a decade" / "this increase explains very little of the rise in the divorce rate over the past half century".
- https://www.nber.org/papers/w10014
- Supporting PRIMARY (Brinig and Allen, 1b): wives were already about 60% of filers in the 19th century.

**6c. It was followed by fewer female suicides and less domestic violence.** PRIMARY (NBER page; fragments).
- Stevenson and Wolfers, "Bargaining in the Shadow of the Law", NBER 10175, 2003. State-by-state timing of the laws. Women's suicide fell; no significant effect on men's suicide; domestic violence fell for both sexes.
- Verbatim: "In sum, we find strong evidence that legal institutions have profound real effects on outcomes within families."
- https://www.nber.org/papers/w10175
- Does NOT show: that no-fault was costless. CONTESTED: other economists find larger or longer effects on divorce and on children; I did not open those papers.

**6d. The divorce rate has been falling for forty years.** PRIMARY (Pew 2025, as 5c).
- Divorces per 1,000 married women: 22.6 around 1980, 20.5 in 2008, 14.4 in 2023. About one in three ever-married adults has seen a first marriage end. Of those who married in the 1970s, 47% had divorced by 2023.

## 7. Prenuptial agreements

**7a. What they can and cannot do.** PRIMARY (statute) and SECONDARY (American Bar Association article, 1 May 2023).
- A prenup can settle property, a business owned before marriage, and in most states can limit or waive alimony. It cannot fix custody or child support in advance.
- Statute, Arizona Revised Statutes 25-203(B), which follows the Uniform Premarital Agreement Act, verbatim: "The right of a child to support may not be adversely affected by a premarital or postnuptial agreement." https://www.azleg.gov/ars/25/00203.htm
- ABA article (FRAGMENT): prenups "cannot predetermine child support or custody time." Courts have thrown agreements out for lack of a lawyer, poor English, or signing too close to the wedding; full financial disclosure and separate lawyers are advised. https://www.americanbar.org/groups/family_law/publications/family-advocate/2023/spring/what-is-prenuptial-agreement/
- Does NOT show: your state's rule. Alimony waivers are treated differently from state to state.

**7b. How many couples have one.** SECONDARY, LOW QUALITY (Axios report of a Harris Poll, September 2023).
- Figures: about 1 in 5 married couples said they had one; 50% of adults said they at least somewhat support prenups (42% in 2022).
- https://axios.com/2023/09/24/prenup-rates-us-marriage
- Weakness: online opt-in poll, no sample size or method given, self-report, commissioned by a news outlet. The same poll's claim that over 40% of young married or engaged adults signed one is implausibly high. Print only as "one poll suggests".

## 8. What lowers a man's divorce risk

**8a. The baseline, and what moves it.** PRIMARY (CDC/NCHS, National Health Statistics Reports no. 49, 2012; National Survey of Family Growth 2006-2010; 10,403 men and 12,279 women aged 15-44).
- Probability a first marriage is still intact at 20 years: men 56%, women 52%.
- Education: men with a bachelor's degree 65% (high school only 47%); women with a degree 78% (high school only 41%).
- Age at marriage, at 10 years: men who married under 20, 48%; at 25 or over, 76%. Women: 54% and 78%.
- Children and timing, men at 20 years: first child born before the marriage 43%; born within 7 months of the wedding 42%; born 8 or more months after 74%. Women: 33%, 41%, 68%.
- Cohabitation, men at 20 years: did not live together first 60%; lived together while engaged 57%; lived together without being engaged 49%.
- Own parents together when he was 14: 60% against 44%.
- https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhsr/nhsr049.pdf
- Does NOT show: cause. These are associations; the people who marry late with degrees differ in many other ways. Sample is under 45, so 20-year figures come only from those who married young.

**8b. College graduates divorce far less.** PRIMARY (Bureau of Labor Statistics, Monthly Labor Review, 2013; people born 1957-1964 followed to age 46).
- First marriages ending in divorce: men with a bachelor's degree 23.7%; men with high school only 48.7%; men who did not finish high school 56.6%. All together 44.2%.
- https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/2013/article/marriage-and-divorce-patterns-by-gender-race-and-educational-attainment.htm
- Pew 2025 (5c) agrees for today: 25% of ever-married graduates have divorced against 37-41% of others.
- Does NOT show: a figure for couples marrying now. No one can know a lifetime rate for a marriage made this year; "about one in four for a graduate" is a past-cohort figure, by age 46.

**8c. A husband without full-time work is at higher risk; her earnings are not the problem.** PRIMARY (the article, on the American Sociological Association site).
- Killewald, "Money, Work, and Marital Stability", American Sociological Review, 2016. Panel Study of Income Dynamics 1968-2013; 6,309 couples, 1,684 divorces. For marriages begun in 1975 or later, the predicted chance of divorce in the next year was 3.3% if the husband was not in full-time work against 2.5% if he was. The wife's economic independence did not predict divorce.
- Verbatim: "Expectations of wives' homemaking may have eroded, but the husband breadwinner norm persists."
- https://www.asanet.org/wp-content/uploads/attach/journals/aug16asrfeature.pdf
- Does NOT show: that money buys safety. The gap is 0.8 points a year, and the paper finds no support for the idea that wives leave because they can afford to.

**8d. Fewer previous partners, lower risk.** SECONDARY (authors' own summaries on the Institute for Family Studies site, an advocacy-leaning institute; the journal article would not open).
- Smith and Wolfinger, Journal of Family Issues, 2023, Add Health survey, men and women: one to eight premarital partners went with about 50% higher odds of divorce than none; nine or more with higher still; the same for men and women; religiosity and attitudes did not explain it away. https://ifstudies.org/blog/testing-common-theories-on-the-relationship-between-premarital-sex-and-marital-stability
- Wolfinger 2016, women only, federal survey: women who married as virgins in the 2000s had a 6% five-year divorce rate; those with ten or more partners 33%. https://ifstudies.org/blog/counterintuitive-trends-in-the-link-between-premarital-sex-and-marital-stability
- CONTESTED: association only; the authors themselves say selection is not ruled out. Whatever makes a person have many partners may also make them leave marriages.

**8e. Shared religious practice.** PRIMARY (PLOS ONE article).
- Li, Kubzansky and VanderWeele, 2018. 66,444 married women in the Nurses' Health Study, 1996-2010. Those attending services more than once a week had a hazard ratio for divorce of 0.58 (CI 0.44-0.74) against never-attenders.
- https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0207778
- Does NOT show: anything about men, about belief as opposed to attendance, or about *shared* practice; mostly white Christian nurses; no data on whether the marriages that lasted were happy. CDC 2012 (8a) offers only "religion raised in", where the differences are small. For an atheist reader the honest version is: the measured effect is of belonging to a community that meets weekly and holds marriage in esteem, not of doctrine. I found no primary source isolating "shared values" among the non-religious.

## 9. The other side of the ledger: men who do not marry

All association, not cause. Men who are poorer, sicker or less employed are less likely to marry in the first place.

**9a. Unpartnered men in mid-life earn less and work less.** PRIMARY (Pew, 5 October 2021, Census data for 2019, ages 25-54).
- Figures: 39% of men were unpartnered. Median earnings $35,600 against $57,000 for partnered men. Employed: 73% against 91%. Degree: 26% against 37%. Living in a parent's home: 31% against 2%.
- Verbatim, on selection versus effect: "The research suggests that both factors are at play."
- https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2021/10/05/rising-share-of-u-s-adults-are-living-without-a-spouse-or-partner.md

**9b. Among unmarried men in late middle age, the never-married are worst off.** PRIMARY (Lin and Brown, The Gerontologist, 2012; 2009 American Community Survey, baby boomers then aged 45-63; article text opened on PubMed Central).
- Figures: never-married men: 20.2% in poverty, 73.0% living alone, 22.5% with a disability, 74.2% insured. Divorced men: 15.7%, 62.8%, 20.8%, 74.9%. Married boomers of both sexes: 4.2% in poverty, 11.1% disabled, 90.1% insured.
- Verbatim: "Never-married men seemed the most disadvantaged, whereas widowed and divorced men fared better."
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3304891
- Note for the argument: on these numbers a divorced man was still somewhat better off than a never-married one.

**9c. Never-married people die sooner, men especially.** SECONDARY (journal's press release, August 2006; the article page gave only the citation).
- Kaplan and Kronick, Journal of Epidemiology and Community Health, 2006. Marital status in 1989, deaths to 1997. Never-married people were 58% more likely to die in the period than the married, after adjustment; the penalty was larger for men; never-married men aged 19-44 were more than twice as likely to die as married men of the same age.
- https://www.eurekalert.org/pub_releases/2006-08/bsj-nmr080806.php
- Does NOT show: that marrying would have saved them.

**9d. Loneliness.** PRIMARY (Pew, 16 January 2025; 6,204 adults, September 2024).
- About 16% of adults feel lonely all or most of the time, men and women about equally; unmarried adults are among the most likely to. Men are much less likely than women to turn to a friend for emotional support (38% against 54%).

## The honest summary

**True in the skeptic's claim**
- Wives ask for about two thirds of divorces (1a, 1b), and the spouse who expects to keep the children is the one who files (1b).
- Mothers are four in five custodial parents (2a); fathers are nine in ten child support payers (3a).
- Judges handed identical files gave mothers more time (2e). The governing legal test is admitted by a leading scholar to give judges little guidance (quote 1).
- A man can be jailed for a debt without a lawyer, and the Supreme Court found it had been done unlawfully (3d). He can be held to support a child who is not his (3e).
- Divorce more than doubles a man's suicide risk (5d), and about a quarter of fathers living apart had not seen their children in a year (5e).

**Exaggerated**
- "Courts take the kids": about 1.5% of cases in the best study were decided by a judge (2b); shared custody rose from 12% to 50% in one state's records (2c); seven states now presume equal time (2d).
- "She gets rich": women's household income falls more and for longer (5a, 5b); custodial mothers' poverty rate is 27% (3b); under half receive the full support ordered.
- "Alimony for life": a few hundred thousand recipients nationally (4a); Florida abolished permanent alimony (4b).
- "No-fault caused it": the effect lasted about a decade (6b); wives were the majority of filers a century earlier (1b); the divorce rate has fallen by a third since 1980 (6d).
- "Half of marriages fail": for a male graduate of the last fully tracked cohort, under a quarter (8b).

**What lowers his risk (associations)**
Marry at 25 or later; finish a degree; keep steady full-time work; do not marry because of a pregnancy; have the first child after the wedding, not before; choose someone whose parents stayed together and who has had few previous partners, and be that person; belong together to some community that meets regularly; sign a prenup for property with separate lawyers, knowing it cannot touch custody or child support; live in, or know the rules of, the state you marry in.
