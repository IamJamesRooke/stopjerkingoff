## 2. DOES "TESTING" WORK?

### 2a. Number of premarital partners and divorce

**2a-1. Wolfinger, Institute for Family Studies, 6 June 2016**
- Claim: Among American women, those who married as virgins had the lowest five-year divorce rates in every decade studied.
- Figures (five-year divorce rate, first marriages, women): no premarital partners: 11% (1980s marriages), 8% (1990s), 6% (2000s). Two partners: 28% (1980s), 30% (2000s). Ten or more partners: 18% (1980s), 33% (2000s). The 33% is "not statistically significantly higher than the 30 percent" for two partners. Figures for exactly one partner and 3-9 partners are only in a chart I could not read.
- Sample: National Survey of Family Growth, 2002, 2006-10, 2011-13 waves; regression Ns 1,899 (1980s), 4,292 (1990s), 3,597 (2000s). Women only.
- URL: https://ifstudies.org/blog/counterintuitive-trends-in-the-link-between-premarital-sex-and-marital-stability (also University of Utah summary, 8 July 2016: https://attheu.utah.edu/facultystaff/premarital-sex-and-divorce/)
- What it does not show: causation. Wolfinger himself: "It's also entirely likely that the correlation is spurious, the product of certain personal characteristics." Controls (including religion) "account for about one quarter of the observed association" at best. The pattern is not a straight line: two partners was as risky as ten or more in some decades. It is a think-tank blog analysis, not peer reviewed. Women only, so it says nothing direct about men's partner counts.
- Confidence: PRIMARY (author's post read). Correlation only.

**2a-2. Smith and Wolfinger, "Re-Examining the Link Between Premarital Sex and Divorce", Journal of Family Issues (published online 2023)**
- Claim: In a national sample followed from adolescence, people with premarital sexual partners were more likely to divorce than those with none, even after controlling for teenage beliefs, religion and family background, and the pattern was the same for men and women.
- Figures (odds ratios versus no premarital partners, fullest model): any premarital partner, full sample: about 2.5. Late-marrying subsample: 1-8 partners 1.64; 9 or more partners 3.20. Quote pieces from abstract: "Premarital sex predicts divorce, but we do not know why." ... the relationship is "highly significant and robust even when accounting for early-life factors." ... "There is no evidence of gender differences."
- Sample: National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health), Waves I (1994-95) to IV (2008-09); 7,030 ever-married respondents; late-marrying subsample 3,506. Respondents were 24-32 at Wave IV.
- URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10989935/ (full text, read to within the last 2,300 characters)
- What it does not show: causation (authors say unmeasured factors may drive it); it covers early marriages and early divorces only ("most reflective of early marriages and divorces"); the partner count measure is incomplete.
- Confidence: PRIMARY, peer reviewed. This is the strongest single item for "applies to men too".

**2a-3. Busby, Carroll and Willoughby, "Compatibility or restraint? The effects of sexual timing on marriage relationships", Journal of Family Psychology 24(6), 2010, DOI 10.1037/a0021690**
- Claim: Married people who waited until marriage to have sex with their spouse reported better marriages than those who had sex early in the relationship.
- Figures: compared with those who became sexual early, those who waited until marriage rated relationship stability 22% higher, satisfaction 20% higher, sexual quality 15% better, communication 12% better. Those who waited longer but not until marriage got about half the benefit. Controlled for religious involvement.
- Sample: 2,035 married individuals from the RELATE online assessment, chosen to match US married demographics.
- Quote (Busby): "Regardless of religiosity, waiting helps the relationship form better communication processes" (may be a fragment of a longer sentence). Also: "There's more to a relationship than sex, but we did find that those who waited longer were happier" ... "with the sexual aspect of their relationship" (joined from two pieces).
- URLs: https://news.byu.edu/news/good-things-come-couples-who-wait (21 Dec 2010) and https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/12/101222112102.htm
- What it does not show: causation. Retrospective self-report from people who chose to take an online marriage questionnaire; only people still married are in it; the authors are at Brigham Young University (a religious university), which critics note. I did not read the paper itself.
- Confidence: SECONDARY (university press release and ScienceDaily). Paper title directly answers the "compatibility" claim: the data favoured restraint.

### 2b. Living together first and divorce

**2b-1. Rosenfeld and Roesler, "Cohabitation Experience and Cohabitation's Association With Marital Dissolution", Journal of Marriage and Family, 2019**
- Claim: Across about four decades of American marriages, couples who lived together before marrying had higher odds of the marriage ending, and that link did not fade over time; the only advantage for those who lived together first was in the first year of marriage.
- Figures: odds of dissolution 1.37 times higher unadjusted (95% CI 1.31-1.43), 1.31 with controls (1.25-1.38). First year of marriage: breakup rate 4.1% for those who had not cohabited versus 3.9% for those who had (raw gap not significant; significant in models). Women who had also lived with a different partner before: odds 3.25 times (one earlier partner) and 4.66 times (two or more).
- Sample: National Survey of Family Growth, six waves 1988 to 2013-15; 24,888 women in first marriages; 216,455 couple-years; 8,488 dissolutions.
- URL: https://web.stanford.edu/~mrosenfe/Rosenfeld_and_Roesler_Cohabitation_Experience_NSFG.pdf
- Authors' own limits: "We make no attempt in this paper to isolate the causal mechanisms at the root of the association." Results "apply to white American women, but the results do not apply in the same way to black women." Women respondents only.
- Confidence: PRIMARY, peer reviewed. CONTESTED by Kuperberg (below) and by Manning, Smock and Kuperberg in a published comment (I saw the reply's appendix listed but did not read the comment itself).

**2b-2. The other side: Kuperberg, "Age at Coresidence, Premarital Cohabitation, and Marriage Dissolution: 1985-2009", Journal of Marriage and Family 76:352-369, 2014; and her Council on Contemporary Families post of 16 October 2018**
- Claim: What predicts divorce is how young a couple were when they first moved in together (married or not), not cohabitation itself; once you compare by age at moving in, the cohabitation penalty disappears.
- Quotes (2018 post, pieces): "accounting for age at marriage but not the age at which couples moved in together artificially inflates the association" ... "the reason premarital cohabitors seem to have a higher risk of divorce is not because cohabitation causes divorce".
- URL: https://thesocietypages.org/ccf/?p=1769 (2018 post, read). The 2014 paper itself and the 2014 press brief were NOT OPENED (redirect), so the often-repeated "wait until 23" figure is NOT verified here; do not print it.
- Rosenfeld and Roesler's reply, 24-27 November 2018 (https://thesocietypages.org/ccf/?p=1817 and https://web.stanford.edu/~mrosenfe/Rosenfeld_Roesler_response_Kuperberg.htm): they agree her method can shrink the association to zero (coefficient 0.13 falling to -0.01) but "we do not agree that this is the best or the only reasonable approach."
- Confidence: PRIMARY for both blog posts. Honest summary: researchers agree the raw link exists; they disagree on whether cohabitation itself adds risk. Nobody in this debate finds that living together first protects a marriage beyond the first year.

**2b-3. Stanley and Rhoades, "What's the Plan? Cohabitation, Engagement, and Divorce", Institute for Family Studies, April 2023**
- Claim: Among Americans who first married in 2010-2019, those who moved in together before being engaged were more likely to have seen the marriage end than those who waited until engagement or marriage; and those who moved in "to test the relationship" did worse than those who moved in to spend more time together.
- Figures: marriage ended for 34% of those who lived together before engagement versus 23% of those who lived together only after engagement or marriage (the report calls this 48% more likely). Adjusted breakdown: before engagement 34%; only after engagement 21%; not before marriage 24%. "Slid" into living together 34% versus "decided" 21% unadjusted; after controls the gap was about 6 points, p = .06 (not statistically significant). 64% of cohabiters said they slid, 36% decided. Reasons: "testing the relationship and finances were statistically associated with greater odds of dissolution" (exact rates only in a figure). About 70% of couples now live together before marriage.
- Sample: YouGov national survey, N = 1,621, fielded 28 July-29 August 2022, first marriages 2010-2019. Controls included age at marriage, education, prior partners, children.
- Quote: "the timing of moving in together is robustly associated with marital instability"; "one key to reducing the risk of divorce may be either not to cohabit before marriage or to have settled the big question" (fragment).
- URLs: https://ifstudies.org/reports/whats-the-plan-cohabitation/2023/executive-summary and https://ifstudies.org/report-brief/whats-the-plan-cohabitation-engagement-and-divorce
- What it does not show: causation; marriages were at most about 12 years old; note that couples who lived together only after engagement did as well as (slightly better than) those who never did, so the finding is about commitment first, not about cohabitation as such. Think-tank report, not a peer-reviewed article (the authors' earlier peer-reviewed work makes the same argument). The Institute for Family Studies is pro-marriage.
- Confidence: PRIMARY (report pages read).

**Bottom line for the chapter:** the test-drive claim predicts that couples who try each other out first should have more durable marriages. No data set opened here shows that. The safest wording: "In study after study, testing first is associated with the same or a higher chance of divorce, never a lower one, except in the first year of marriage. Researchers argue over why."

### 3b. What marriage is associated with for men

- Happiness: 2022 General Social Survey, ages 18-55, Wilcox and Wang, IFS, 12 September 2023: 35% of married fathers and 30% of married childless men said they were "very happy"; under 15% of unmarried men (with or without children). "Married men (ages 18-55) in America are about twice as likely to be very happy, compared to their unmarried peers." URL: https://ifstudies.org/blog/who-is-happiest-married-mothers-and-fathers-per-the-latest-general-social-survey PRIMARY for the post. Association only.
- Money (Wilcox, "Get Married", 2024, as reported in Deseret News, 15 December 2024, Betsy VanDenBerghe): household income of married men in their thirties about $95,000 versus $68,000 cohabiting and $42,000 single; "married men have 40% more household income than unmarried men" after controls; "55% less likely to live in poverty". URL: https://www.deseret.com/opinion/2024/12/15/marriage-has-enormous-benefits/ SECONDARY (book not opened; no page numbers; household income is partly two earners, so this is not "he earns more").
- Wealth: Pew Research Center, 16 October 2025: median household wealth $326,900 for working-age adults in a first marriage, $98,700 divorced, $32,600 never married. URL: https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/10/16/8-facts-about-divorce-in-the-united-states/ PRIMARY. Household, not per person.
- Health and long life: Harvard Study of Adult Development, Robert Waldinger's TED talk "What Makes a Good Life?" (recorded November 2015; the study followed 724 men from 1938): "The people who were the most satisfied in their relationships at age 50, were the healthiest at age 80." and "Good relationships keep us happier and healthier, period." Text from a subtitle file: https://amara.org/subtitles/C12rlObrNft1/en/34/download/... Same line on NPR TED Radio Hour (2016): "good relationships keep us happier and healthier. Period." https://www.npr.org/transcripts/481401234 PRIMARY (speaker's own words; subtitle file is community-edited, so check punctuation against ted.com). Note the finding is about good relationships, not marriage as a legal status.
- Lifespan figure for married men: nothing verified this session. Do not print a number.

### 3c. The honest other side

- Divorce today: refined divorce rate 14.2 divorces per 1,000 married women aged 15+ in 2024 (14.4 in 2023), National Center for Family & Marriage Research, Bowling Green State University, from the American Community Survey. https://www.bgsu.edu/ncfmr/resources/data/family-profiles/fp-25-31.html PRIMARY. Pew (2025): the rate peaked around 1980 at 22.6; of Americans who married in the 1970s, 47% had divorced; 1980s, 46%; "One-third" of all ever-married Americans in 2023 had seen a first marriage end in divorce ("This does *not* mean that 33% of marriages end in divorce."). Four in ten divorces happen in the first ten years. So "half of marriages fail" is out of date for recent marriages, but the risk is real: roughly one in three to four in ten on current evidence. Do not print a single "lifetime divorce risk" percentage; none was verified.
- Selection: Lucas, Clark, Georgellis and Diener, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 84(3), 2003: over 24,000 people in Germany followed 15 years; marriage raised life satisfaction by about one-tenth of a point on a 0-10 scale and the lift faded; people who married and stayed married were already more satisfied before the wedding. APA press release 16 March 2003: https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2003/03/married-happy SECONDARY (press release). Also sociologist David Weakliem, quoted on Andrew Gelman's blog 27 August 2024: "the difference in happiness between married and never-married women is not necessarily caused by marriage." https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2024/08/27/marriage-happiness/ Summary: happier, healthier, better-off men are more likely to marry and stay married; part of the "marriage benefit" is who marries. Wilcox argues a real effect remains; critics argue it is small. State as association.
- What a bad marriage costs: Holt-Lunstad, Birmingham and Jones, Annals of Behavioral Medicine, 2008: 204 married and 99 single adults wore blood pressure monitors for 24 hours; happily married adults scored four points lower than singles; unhappily married adults had higher blood pressure than both. Holt-Lunstad: "It's not just being married that benefits health - what's really the most protective of health is having a happy marriage." https://news.byu.edu/news/happily-marrieds-have-lower-blood-pressure-social-singles-byu-study-shows SECONDARY (press release, 12 March 2008). Waldinger (2015): "High conflict marriages for example, without much affection, turn out to be very bad for our health, perhaps worst than getting divorced." (joined from two pieces; "worst" is how the subtitle file spells it; the spoken word is almost certainly "worse", check ted.com). Divorce cost, Pew 2025: median household income $84,900 for divorced working-age adults versus $118,600 in a first marriage; 73% of divorced men employed versus 88% of men in a first marriage (Pew notes unemployed men are more likely to divorce, so direction is unclear).

### 3d. What predicts a lasting marriage that a man can look for

- Contempt: Gottman Institute blog, Eva Van Prooyen, 25 August 2017: "The existence of contempt is the biggest predictor of divorce." The four warning signs "are criticism, contempt, defensiveness, and stonewalling." https://www.gottman.com/blog/this-one-thing-is-the-biggest-predictor-of-divorce/ PRIMARY for the Institute's own statement. CONTESTED: the "over 90% accuracy" claim comes from fitting models to couples whose outcomes were already known; other researchers (for example Heyman and Slep, 2001) have criticised calling that prediction. I did not open the critique this session; do not print the 90% figure.
- Age at marriage: Wolfinger, IFS, 17 July 2015, National Survey of Family Growth 2006-10: each extra year of age at marriage up to about 32 lowers the odds of divorce by 11%; after about 32 the odds rise 5% a year. "For almost everyone, the late twenties seems to be the best time to tie the knot." https://ifstudies.org/blog/want-to-avoid-divorce-wait-to-get-married-but-not-too-long/ PRIMARY for the post; the author calls his explanation "conjecture" and other analysts have disputed the after-32 rise. Safe part: marrying as a teenager or in the very early twenties carries clearly higher divorce risk.
- Commit before you move in: Stanley and Rhoades 2023 (2b-3): 34% versus 23%.
- Decide, do not slide: same report, 34% versus 21% unadjusted, not significant after controls. State it that way.
- Fewer earlier live-in partners: Rosenfeld and Roesler 2019 (odds 3.25 and 4.66 times); Stanley and Rhoades 2023 ("The group at highest risk for dissolution were those who cohabited with two or more prior partners.").
- Education and stable work: Pew 2025: 25% of ever-married college graduates have divorced versus 37% (high school or less) and 41% (some college).
- "Shared values / shared faith": no primary source verified this session. Leave as the author's own argument, not as a statistic.

## THE EIGHT STRONGEST, SAFEST ITEMS

1. Smith and Wolfinger (Journal of Family Issues, 2023; Add Health, 7,030 ever-married people): premarital partners predicted divorce after controls; odds about 1.6 times (1-8 partners) and 3.2 times (9+) versus none; "There is no evidence of gender differences." Correlation; early marriages only. PRIMARY.
2. Stanley and Rhoades (IFS, 2023; N = 1,621; marriages 2010-2019): 34% of marriages ended among those who lived together before engagement versus 23% among those who did so only after engagement or marriage; moving in "to test the relationship" was linked to higher odds of the marriage ending. Correlation. PRIMARY.
3. Rosenfeld and Roesler (Journal of Marriage and Family, 2019; 24,888 women, 1970-2015 marriages): living together first linked to 1.31 times the odds of the marriage ending after controls, with an advantage only in the first year. Give Kuperberg's objection (age at moving in) alongside. PRIMARY, CONTESTED.
