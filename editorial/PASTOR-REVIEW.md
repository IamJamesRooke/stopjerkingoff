# Pastors' editorial pass

Written 2026-11-19 from a full read of `main` at commit 3ff23bc (after the Chapter 9 split). It reads the book the way a careful Catholic priest or a mainstream Protestant pastor would, before James sends it to clergy. The title is out of scope.

Nothing in the chapters was changed. Chapter work goes through the Author. Every suggested wording below follows the standing rules: it reads well aloud, it's plain American English, it explains each reference in place, and it never sends the reader back or forward.

## The short version

The book is in good shape for clergy. Every Bible quotation checks out against the World English Bible, and the new Chapter 9 tells all three stories faithfully. The grace notes are strong: Romans 8:1 beside the call to repent, the line between desire and lust, the Pharisee and the tax collector, the wives' section, and the prayer in Chapter 28, which says "I can't keep this by my own strength." A pastor will like far more than he dislikes.

But five things would stop a priest or pastor from recommending it. Each can be fixed in a sentence or a paragraph, without losing the method.

1. **"Carry on as normal" tells a man to keep sinning, and to delay repenting.** This is the big one. Chapter 21 even says, "don't make your final decision until you finish the book."
2. **In a few places the method takes the credit that belongs to grace.** This is the "salvation by technique" worry, in half a dozen specific lines.
3. **It promises there will be no lifelong struggle.** Both traditions teach that a Christian stays watchful for life, and the book's own epigraphs say so.
4. **It never mentions Confession.** A Catholic priest will notice this at once.
5. **It never says plainly that sex belongs in marriage.** The "third face" is treated as bad judgment and "using", never as sex outside marriage.

After those come 18 medium items and a list of small ones.

## How each finding is laid out

Each finding gives the chapter, the line as it stands, the problem, who would object, and a suggested fix. "Both" means a Catholic priest and a mainstream Protestant pastor would both object. Severity: **High** = would stop a pastor recommending the book. **Medium** = a pastor would write it in the margin. **Low** = polish.

Chapter numbers are the new ones (Chapter 9 is "A City Without Walls").

## HIGH: must fix before sending to clergy

### H1. "Carry on as normal" and "don't decide until the end"

**Where:** Introduction ("Live as you are and keep reading to the last page"); Chapter 1 ("Until you've finished the whole book, carry on as normal... Live as you've been living"); Chapter 17 (epigraph "Behold, now is the day of salvation" in the chapter that says stop when you finish); Chapter 21, "Two warnings" ("**First: don't make your final decision until you finish the book.** I know. You're ready now... Until then, carry on as normal"); Chapter 32 ("Until the last page your only instructions are to keep reading and to carry on as normal"); FAQ ("Do I have to stop while I'm reading? No.").

**Problem:** The book explains carefully that this isn't permission to sin, and its reason is sound: a willpower vow halfway through usually fails. But the wording still instructs the reader to go on doing what the book calls sin, and in Chapter 21 it tells a man who is ready to repent to wait. To a pastor, that is the one thing a Christian book can't say. Paul's answer to "shall we continue in sin?" is "May it never be!" (Romans 6:1-2). Hebrews says "Today if you will hear his voice, don't harden your hearts" (Hebrews 3:15). Chapter 17's own epigraph says "now is the day of salvation", and then the chapter says "not yet". Chapter 21 quotes 1 John 1:9 at the top and then says don't confess until Chapter 28.

**Who objects:** Both. A Catholic priest will read it as an invitation to sin with the intention of continuing, which removes the "firm purpose of amendment" that real contrition needs (Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1451). A Protestant pastor will read it as the Romans 6:1 error.

**Fix:** Keep the method's real point, which is "don't start a willpower campaign, and don't despair if you fall while reading." Drop every line that tells him to keep doing it or to wait.

- Chapter 1, replace "Until you've finished the whole book, carry on as normal. / Don't start a streak. Don't make a vow. Don't cut down to weekends. Live as you've been living." with:

  > Until you've finished the whole book, don't start a willpower campaign. Don't start a streak. Don't make a vow you'll break by Friday. Don't cut down to weekends. If you fall while you're reading, don't despair, and don't stop reading. And if the wish leaves you along the way, let it go, and don't call it back.

- Chapter 1, rename the heading "The instruction nobody likes" to "No willpower campaign", and change "Nobody is ordering you to do it" to "Nobody is asking you to do it. This book will never tell you to sin."

- Chapter 21, replace the first of the "Two warnings" with:

  > **First: if you're ready now, don't wait for my permission.** Turn now. You can pray the prayer in Chapter 28 today. Then keep reading to the end anyway, because the chapters still ahead are what keep a man from sliding back.

- Introduction: change "Live as you are and keep reading to the last page" to "Don't fight it with willpower while you read. Just keep reading to the last page."
- Chapter 32: "Until the last page your only instructions are to keep reading and to carry on as normal" becomes "Until the last page, your only instruction is to keep reading."
- FAQ: rewrite the answer to "Do I have to stop while I'm reading?" to match the Chapter 1 wording above.
- Chapter 28 opening: "If you've carried on as normal while you read, your last time is already behind you" becomes "Whenever your last time was, it's behind you now."

This touches the method's signature line, so it's James's call. But no priest or pastor will hand the book to a parishioner with "carry on as normal" in it.

### H2. Places where the method takes the credit that belongs to grace

The book mostly gets this right. The prayer in Chapter 28 depends on grace, and Chapter 11 rightly says Paul's answer is "a person and a gift." But a handful of lines say the book, the reader's decision or a change of mind is what frees a man. Those are the lines a pastor will underline.

| Chapter | Line as it stands | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | "This book is that button. It just takes a few hours to press." | Presents the book as the cure. |
| Introduction | "I'd wish you luck, but luck doesn't come into it." | The natural Christian answer is grace, and the line leaves it out. |
| 1 | "call it changing your mind, which is the only thing that has ever changed a man." | Grace changes a man. In Romans 12:2 "be transformed" is passive: it is done to you, by God. |
| 2 | "in John 8:32, Jesus gave the whole method in a single line... You don't have to believe who said it to notice that it works." | Proof-texting. The verse before it says "If you remain in my word, then you are truly my disciples." The truth is Jesus himself (John 14:6), and verse 36 says it is the Son who makes you free. |
| 11 | "If you're not a believer, stay with me, because the logic holds without the theology... That's what this book is doing, a chapter at a time." | It comes straight after "who will deliver me?... The answer is a person." It then hands the job of deliverance back to the book. |
| 21 | "Decide and rejoice, or repent and receive. That's the whole method." and the heading "You're free the moment you decide" | Repentance is presented as a human decision. Both traditions teach that repentance is itself a gift of grace (Acts 11:18). |
| 28 | "That's what you're after, and it's entirely in your power... You didn't become that man gradually. You became him when you said it." | Self-transformation by a spoken word. It also contradicts Chapter 27, which rightly says freedom can come "like a dawn". |

**Who objects:** Both. For a Protestant this is the heart of the gospel: "by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God" (Ephesians 2:8), and "it is God who works in you both to will and to work" (Philippians 2:13). A Catholic priest will call it Pelagian, the old error that a man can make himself good without grace. The Church condemned it in the fifth and sixth centuries.

**Fix:** Add one framing paragraph early, and adjust the lines above.

- Introduction, at the end of "A word on faith, and a word to men", after "Guilt has never set a man free":

  > One more thing, so that nobody misreads this book. Nothing in it saves anyone. Freedom is God's gift, and only he gives it. What this book can do is clear away the lies that keep a man from taking that gift. Paul calls that "the renewing of your mind". If you're not a believer, the lies still need clearing, and the book will do that for you too.

- Introduction: "This book is that button. It just takes a few hours to press." becomes "This book can't press it for you. But it can show you that the button is real, and that it's closer than you think."
- Introduction: "I'd wish you luck, but luck doesn't come into it." becomes "I won't wish you luck, because luck doesn't come into it. Grace does."
- Chapter 1: "He didn't say to try harder. He said to be transformed by seeing things differently. If you're not a believer, call it changing your mind, which is the only thing that has ever changed a man." becomes:

  > He didn't say to try harder. He said "be transformed", which is something done to you, by God, as your mind is made new. If you're not a believer, call it changing your mind. Either way, it starts with seeing clearly.

- Chapter 2, last paragraph: replace "Jesus gave the whole method in a single line: 'You will know the truth, and the truth will make you free.' You don't have to believe who said it to notice that it works." with:

  > Jesus said, "You will know the truth, and the truth will make you free." He was talking to people who had started to follow him, and the truth he meant was his own word. A few verses later he says it is the Son who makes a man free. This book is about one small corner of that truth: the lies lust tells. If you're not a believer, you can still test those lies for yourself.

- Chapter 11: keep "IT ENDS WHEN ONE OF THEM IS GONE", but change the lead-in to: "If you're not a believer, stay with me, because part of this holds without the theology." Then end the paragraph with: "For a believer, that is the work Paul gave thanks for. It is done by the one who delivers, and this book is only clearing the ground for it."
- Chapter 21: "That's the whole method" becomes "That's the whole method, and both halves are gifts. God gives the turning, and God gives the grace." Rename "You're free the moment you decide" to "Free from the first day". In that section, add after "You become one in that moment": "Not because your decision is strong, but because of what Christ has already done."
- Chapter 28: "That's what you're after, and it's entirely in your power" becomes "That's what you're after, and it's within reach". Change "You didn't become that man gradually. You became him when you said it." to "You don't have to wait to become that man. You started being him when you said it, and God finishes what he starts."

### H3. "No lifelong struggle"

**Where:** Introduction ("**Permanent.**" in the promises list; "They don't describe a lifelong fight"); Chapter 11 ("The men's breakfast at church says so, and adds that you'll be fighting this until the day you die" and "the old saint who says he's battled it for fifty years", both given as lies); Chapter 6 ("You simply won't want them"); Chapter 27 ("you'll live the rest of your life without it and never want it back").

**Problem:** It is fair to say the habit's grip can break at once. It is not fair to say temptation will never come back, or to mock older Christians who say watchfulness is lifelong. The book's own Chapter 3 epigraph says "the flesh lusts against the Spirit" (Galatians 5:17), in a letter to Christians. Paul warns, "let him who thinks he stands be careful that he doesn't fall" (1 Corinthians 10:12). Peter, quoted in Fit for Service, begins that same verse with "Be sober and self-controlled. Be watchful" (1 Peter 5:8). The book's own Chapter 28 "final warning" admits that free men do go back in. So the Introduction promises more than the book itself believes.

**Who objects:** Both. The Catholic Catechism says self-mastery "is a long and exacting work. One can never consider it acquired once and for all" (2342). It also says the baptized "must continue to struggle against concupiscence of the flesh and disordered desires" (2520). Lutheran and Reformed pastors read Romans 7 as the struggle of a mature Christian. Luther's first thesis was that the whole life of a believer is repentance.

**Fix:** Draw the line between the trap (which can end at once) and watchfulness (which never ends, but stops being misery).

- Introduction promises: "**Permanent.**" becomes "**Lasting.** You don't have to keep walking out of the same trap."
- Introduction: "They don't describe a lifelong fight. They describe chains breaking and a fog lifting." becomes "They don't describe a miserable fight that never ends. They describe chains breaking and a fog lifting. They still keep watch, as every Christian does. But keeping watch is a long way from fighting for your life every night."
- Chapter 11 opening: replace "and adds that you'll be fighting this until the day you die" with "and adds that it will be misery for the rest of your life". Later, replace "He's heard about the old saint who says he's battled it for fifty years" with "He's heard about the man who says it has been a nightly war for fifty years". Then add one sentence near the end of the chapter:

  > The old Christians who say a man must keep watch his whole life are right. Peter said, "Be sober and self-controlled. Be watchful." But a watchman isn't a man wrestling a thing on the other side of the door every night. He's a man who knows what's out there and isn't afraid of it.

- Chapter 27: "You know that you'll live the rest of your life without it and never want it back" becomes "You know you're done with it, and you don't want it back."

### H4. Confession is never mentioned

**Where:** The whole book. There is no mention of the sacrament of Confession, a priest, or Communion. Chapter 29 says, "You've already told God, and that's the confession that counts most." The "Tell one man" advice lists "A pastor, an elder, or an older friend." The Appendix lists "Your pastor or an elder."

**Problem:** For a Catholic, masturbation and pornography are serious sins (the Catechism calls them grave matter, 2352 and 2354). The ordinary way a Catholic is forgiven a serious sin is by confessing it to a priest in the sacrament of Confession, and he should do that before receiving Communion (1456-1457). A Catholic priest reading "that's the confession that counts most" will think the book is quietly telling his people to skip the sacrament. None of this costs a Protestant reader anything.

**Who objects:** Catholic (and Orthodox, and many Anglicans and Lutherans, who keep private confession too).

**Fix:** Add a sentence in four places.

- Chapter 28, after the non-praying version, before "Then stand up and say it":

  > If you're Catholic, take this to Confession as well, and soon. The Church asks Catholics to bring sins like this to a priest. Many men say that was the moment the weight came off. Your priest has heard it all before, and he is bound never to repeat a word of it.

- Chapter 29, "Should I tell my wife or my pastor?": replace "You've already told God, and that's the confession that counts most." with "You've already told God, and that matters most. If you're Catholic, that means Confession too." Under "Tell one man", change the list to "A pastor or a priest, an elder, or an older friend with some sense."
- Chapter 29b, "Confess it today": add "If you're Catholic, that means Confession, and don't put it off."
- Appendix, "Getting ordinary help": change "Your pastor or an elder" to "Your pastor, priest or an elder."

### H5. Sex outside marriage is never named

**Where:** Introduction and Chapter 3 (the "third face"); Chapter 4 ("It's only a little fun. She knew what it was."); Chapter 7 ("None of it was courtship"); Chapter 14 ("He goes from bed to bed and calls it success"); Chapter 29, "What about sex?" ("Within marriage, sex is a gift... If you're single, the answer isn't to go looking for somebody to use in place of your hand").

**Problem:** The book implies the Christian sexual ethic everywhere ("Sex with your wife is the meal"), but never says it outright. Every argument against sex outside marriage is about "using" someone. So a man who sleeps with a girlfriend he loves, with no using in it, could finish the book thinking he's fine. A pastor will notice that a book about lust never says what the Bible says about sex outside marriage.

**Who objects:** Both (Catholic Catechism 2353; evangelical, Baptist, Pentecostal, Reformed and most mainstream Protestant teaching).

**Fix:** One plain sentence with a verse, in Chapter 29, "What about sex?":

> This book has nothing against sex. It's against a counterfeit of it. The Bible keeps sex for marriage: "Let marriage be held in honor among all, and let the bed be undefiled." That line is from the letter to the Hebrews, in the New Testament. Within marriage, sex is a gift, and Chapter 22 told the married man not to hold back from his wife. If you're single, that means waiting, and it means not going looking for somebody to use in place of your hand. That's the same lust, only now there's a victim.

Optionally, add a line to the Introduction's "third face" paragraph: "He sleeps with a woman he isn't married to and tells himself it means nothing, or that it means everything."

## MEDIUM: a pastor would write these in the margin

### M1. Chapter 1 and Chapter 28: Matthew 5:34 used against vows and resolutions
**Line:** "Jesus had no time for that kind of promise. In the Sermon on the Mount... he said, 'don't swear at all'." Chapter 28 repeats it in "A word about vows".
**Problem:** In Matthew 5 Jesus is talking about oaths used to prop up honesty, not resolutions made to God. Scripture is full of good resolutions: "I have sworn, and have confirmed it, that I will obey your righteous ordinances" (Psalm 119:106). The book praises Job's "covenant with my eyes" in Chapter 23, which is a vow. Catholics pray an Act of Contrition that says "I firmly resolve, with the help of your grace, to sin no more", and they take marriage and religious vows. The Westminster Shorter Catechism calls repentance turning to God "with full purpose of, and endeavor after, new obedience."
**Who:** Both, Catholics most.
**Fix:** Chapter 1: replace the Matthew 5 sentences with "What this book rules out is a promise made in your own strength, before you understand the trap. You've made that kind of promise before, and you know how long it lasts. The plain No at the end of this book is a different thing. You'll say it with your eyes open, and you'll say it leaning on God." In Chapter 28, keep "let your 'Yes' be 'Yes' and your 'No' be 'No'" as a picture of plain speech, and drop "Jesus told his followers not to swear oaths at all".

### M2. Chapter 11: "His will was fine"
**Line:** "It wasn't weakness. He changed his mind... His will was fine. He was acting on false information."
**Problem:** This makes sin pure ignorance. That contradicts Chapter 2, where Jesus calls the sinner a "bondservant of sin". It also contradicts Augustine's account of the divided will in *Confessions*, Book 8, which the Appendix recommends.
**Who:** Both.
**Fix:** "It wasn't only weakness. He changed his mind. ... His will was tired, but it wasn't the whole story. He was also acting on false information, and that part this book can fix."

### M3. Chapter 3: "He says nothing about what to push against"
**Line:** "He tells you what to walk toward. He says nothing about what to push against."
**Problem:** It isn't true of Paul. Seven verses later, in the same chapter, he writes, "Those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh with its passions and lusts" (Galatians 5:24). He also says "Flee sexual immorality!" (1 Corinthians 6:18) and "put to death the deeds of the body" (Romans 8:13). The book itself quotes "Flee from youthful lusts" in Chapter 25. A pastor will catch this one.
**Who:** Both.
**Fix:** "In this passage he puts the weight on what to walk toward. Elsewhere he does say to put sin to death, but notice how he says it: 'if by the Spirit you put to death the deeds of the body, you will live.' That's from his letter to the Romans. The Spirit does the heavy lifting."

### M4. Chapter 8: the shadow lifts through confession, not only stopping
**Line:** Psalm 32:3, "When I kept silence, my bones wasted away", followed by the gains of stopping.
**Problem:** Two verses later David gives the remedy: "I acknowledged my sin to you... and you forgave the iniquity of my sin" (Psalm 32:5). The chapter quotes the illness and leaves out the cure, and the cure is the most Christian thing in the psalm.
**Who:** Both.
**Fix:** After the Psalm 32:3 quote, add: "Then he tells you what lifted it: 'I acknowledged my sin to you. I didn't hide my iniquity... and you forgave the iniquity of my sin.' The shadow goes the same way for you. You tell the truth, and you are forgiven."

### M5. Chapter 9: show David's repentance
**Line:** "David repented, and God forgave him."
**Problem:** This is accurate, but it is the most important beat in the story for a pastor, and it is told in five words. In the Bible, David's words are "I have sinned against Yahweh," and Nathan answers, "Yahweh also has put away your sin" (2 Samuel 12:13). Jewish and Christian tradition both link Psalm 51 to this moment, and Psalm 51 is the penitential psalm of both traditions. The book already quotes Psalm 51 in Chapter 32, without context.
**Who:** Both.
**Fix:** "David didn't argue. He said, 'I have sinned against the LORD.' And Nathan told him, 'The LORD also has put away your sin.' Tradition says David wrote Psalm 51 after that day. It begins, 'Have mercy on me, God, according to your loving kindness.' He was forgiven. But the child died, and David's family was never at peace again." (See M16 on "the LORD".)

### M6. Chapter 9: the Jezebel sentence
**Line:** "The first book of Kings, which tells her own story, never shows her seducing anybody."
**Problem:** That's true of 1 Kings, but her story finishes in 2 Kings. There, before she dies, "she painted her eyes, and adorned her head" (2 Kings 9:30), and Jehu speaks of "the prostitution of your mother Jezebel and her witchcraft" (9:22). Most scholars read "prostitution" there as idolatry, but those verses are where the popular picture also comes from, and a pastor will know them.
**Who:** Both (teaching pastors especially).
**Fix:** "The books of Kings, which tell her own story, show an idolater and a ruthless schemer. When she's about to die, she paints her eyes and does her hair, and that scene, along with the last book of the Bible, is where the seductress picture comes from. But Kings never shows her seducing anybody. It shows a ruthless woman and a husband with no will of his own."

### M7. Chapter 9: small liberties in the Uriah story
**Line:** "He never opened it, because he was loyal."
**Problem:** The Bible doesn't say why he didn't open it, or even that he didn't. It's a small thing, but this chapter is new and James asked for faithful retelling.
**Fix:** "As far as the Bible tells us, he carried it straight to the general, unopened. He was loyal to the end."

### M8. Chapter 24: Matthew 5:45 "rain on the just and the unjust"
**Line:** Epigraph and "It rains on the free man and on the man in the trap. You can't have hills without valleys."
**Problem:** In Matthew 5, rain is a blessing. Jesus is saying God is kind even to his enemies, so love yours. The chapter turns rain into bad days, which reverses the verse.
**Who:** Both (any preacher who has preached Matthew 5).
**Fix:** Use Ecclesiastes 7:14 instead. It is exactly the point: "In the day of prosperity be joyful, and in the day of adversity consider; yes, God has made the one side by side with the other." In the body: "The book of Ecclesiastes says God has made the good day and the bad day side by side. They come to the free man and the man in the trap alike." Keep "You can't have hills without valleys."

### M9. Fit for Service: Isaiah "made no speech about his record"
**Line:** "Isaiah had just been forgiven, and he made no speech about his record. He said, 'Here I am. Send me!'"
**Problem:** Isaiah did make a speech about his record first: "Woe is me! For I am undone, because I am a man of unclean lips" (Isaiah 6:5). Then an angel touched his lips with a burning coal, his sin was forgiven, and only after that did he say "Send me." The real order of the story is confession, then cleansing, then sending. That's exactly the order of this book's last chapters, so telling it faithfully makes the point stronger.
**Who:** Both.
**Fix:** "The prophet Isaiah once saw a vision of God on his throne. His first words were, 'Woe is me!', because he knew what he was. Then an angel touched his lips with a coal from the altar and told him his sin was forgiven. And when God asked, 'Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?', the forgiven man didn't hesitate. He said, 'Here I am. Send me!'"

### M10. Fit for Service: "Sobriety is the most important thing in your life, without exception"
**Problem:** For a Christian, God comes first. The book itself quotes "seek first God's Kingdom" in Chapter 20. The book softens the quote ("First doesn't mean building your life around it"), but the quote still says "without exception".
**Who:** Both, mildly.
**Fix:** Drop the quotation and keep the point: "The people who know most about getting sober learned this the hard way. Whatever you put ahead of your sobriety, they say, you will lose. For a Christian, God comes first. But getting sober is how a man gets back to putting him there."

### M11. Chapter 26: near occasions of sin and "custody of the eyes"
**Line:** "The first is the fortress. This man bans the internet, avoids the beach, and looks at his shoes when a woman walks past."
**Problem:** Catholics pray in the Act of Contrition to "avoid the near occasion of sin". Guarding the eyes ("custody of the eyes") is an old and respected Christian practice, and the book praises it in Job in Chapter 23. The point of the passage, that fear-driven avoidance is the Willpower Method, is fair. But a priest will hear his tradition being mocked.
**Who:** Catholic, and many Protestants.
**Fix:** Change "and looks at his shoes when a woman walks past" to "and lives as if every woman were a danger". Then, after "The word to notice is *unnecessary*", add: "Christians have an old phrase for this: avoid the near occasion of sin. It means don't stand where you know you'll fall. That's not fear. It's sense."

### M12. Chapter 29b: "Trying to add your own suffering to the bill isn't humility"
**Problem:** The target, self-punishment that hopes to earn forgiveness, is right. But Catholics practice penance, are given a penance in Confession, and "offer up" suffering. A priest will read this sentence as an attack on penance.
**Who:** Catholic.
**Fix:** "Brooding and self-punishment aren't humility. The debt was paid in full, by Christ. (If you're Catholic and your priest gives you a penance, that's a different thing. It's medicine for the soul, not a payment you add to the bill.)"

### M13. Chapter 31: "Your no is yours. You don't owe him sex"
**Problem:** The protection of a betrayed wife is right, and most pastors will agree with it. But a conservative pastor will think at once of 1 Corinthians 7:3-5 ("Don't deprive one another... that Satan doesn't tempt you because of your lack of self-control"). He may write the section off if the book seems not to know the passage.
**Who:** Conservative Protestant and some Catholic readers.
**Fix:** Show that you know the passage, without weakening her protection: "**Your no is yours.** Paul tells husbands and wives not to deprive each other, and he says it to both of them alike. But that is a gift between two people. It is never a debt he can collect, least of all after he has broken your trust. Being tired, sick or hurt is never his excuse. If closeness comes back, it comes at your speed."

### M14. Masturbation and Catholic teaching (Chapter 4 and the FAQ)
**Line:** Chapter 4: "the Bible never mentions masturbation by name... Ask yourself how often it has happened without lust." FAQ, "Is masturbation a sin?": the answer talks about lust but never answers the question.
**Problem:** This is honest, and it works for most Protestants. But the Catholic Church teaches that masturbation is wrong in itself, whatever is in the mind, because sex is meant to unite husband and wife and be open to life (Catechism 2352). A priest will notice the book argues only from the look. The FAQ's non-answer reads as dodging.
**Who:** Catholic (and the FAQ, both).
**Fix:** Chapter 4, after "you have your answer", add: "The Catholic Church goes further. It teaches that the act is wrong in itself, because sex was made to join a husband and wife. Many Protestant pastors reach the same place through Jesus's words about the look." FAQ: open the answer with "Yes. The book says so plainly, because lust is sin, and for almost every man the two go together." Then keep the rest.

### M15. Chapter 32: The Instructions leave out God
**Problem:** The page the reader carries away is all method. Prayer, Scripture and church appear only as optional verses. Chapter 33's "What goes in the space" is good, but the instruction list is what men will print and keep.
**Who:** Both.
**Fix:** Add a twelfth instruction, or put this before number 11: "Stay close to God. Pray, read the Bible, and be part of a church. You aren't guarding a door. You're getting to know the one who opened it."

### M16. "Yahweh"
**Where:** Nine times in the text: Psalm 4:8 (Ch 5), Judges 16:20 and 16:28 and 1 Kings 21:25 (Ch 9), Numbers 6:24-26 (Ch 33, three times), Psalm 34:18 (Appendix), and "Yahweh" explained in Ch 5.
**Problem:** In 2008 the Vatican asked that the name "Yahweh" not be used in Catholic prayer, song or worship, out of reverence for the divine name. Jewish readers avoid saying it too. The blessing in Chapter 33 is offered as a prayer, which is exactly the case the Vatican letter covers. It also matters for the audiobook, because the narrator has to say it aloud.
**Who:** Catholic (and Jewish readers, and many Protestants who are simply used to "the LORD").
**Fix:** Print "the LORD" in those verses, as nearly every English Bible does. I believe the WEB's own British Edition already prints "the LORD", so check its wording before switching. Add a line to the Attribution page: "The divine name is given as 'the LORD', as in most English Bibles." Then drop the "Yahweh" explanation in Chapter 5.

### M17. Chapter 24 and Chapter 31: clergy are the readers now
**Where:** Chapter 24, "The man at the front of the church", and Chapter 31, "To pastors".
**Problem:** The pastors' section is short and Protestant only ("the men's breakfast"). The leader's section never tells a pastor who is caught what to do, and he is the reader most likely to be caught alone. Since James is sending the book to pastors and priests, these two sections are what they'll turn to first.
**Who:** Both.
**Fix:**
- Chapter 24, add at the end of the section: "If that's you, tell someone outside your own church: a pastor you trust in another town, your bishop or superintendent, a counselor. If you're a priest, tell your confessor or spiritual director. You've spent years carrying other men's secrets. You're allowed to put this one down."
- Chapter 31: retitle "To pastors" as "To pastors and priests", and add: "If you hear confessions, you already know how often this comes through the grille. A man who confesses the same sin every month may need more than absolution and a penance. He may need someone to explain the trap. This book is one way to do that." And: "This book is not a replacement for you. It clears away the lies so that a man can hear what you've been telling him all along."

### M18. Further reading is all Protestant and secular
**Problem:** The list has Lewis (Anglican), Lambert, Chalmers and Augustine, plus Carr and Maltz. A priest will notice there is no Catholic title, even though the book quotes Jason Evert and a Benedictine.
**Who:** Catholic.
**Fix:** Add one: "**The Catechism of the Catholic Church, paragraphs 2331 to 2400.** The Catholic Church's teaching on chastity, short and free to read online." Or Pope John Paul II's *Love and Responsibility*.

## LOW: polish

1. **Chapter 2, Augustine.** It's a missed gift. The verse Augustine read at his conversion was Romans 13:14, "put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh". That's the epigraph of Chapter 12. Chapter 12 could tell the story in four sentences: he heard a child's voice say "Take up and read", opened Paul's letter at that verse, and was free. It ties Chapter 2's "only not yet" to its answer, and both traditions love the story.
2. **Fit for Service, 1 Peter 5:8.** The verse begins "Be sober and self-controlled. Be watchful." Quoting the whole verse fits the drunk-soldier picture perfectly, and it helps H3.
3. **Chapter 23, stale pointer.** "King David took one long look from a rooftop... You'll hear the rest of that story before the book is done." Chapter 9 now tells it before Chapter 23. Change to "You heard the rest of that story in Chapter 9."
4. **Chapter 21, Chalmers.** "Freedom is a new affection, and so is a clean conscience. Christ is the greatest one there is." For Chalmers the new affection was love for God himself, not freedom. Try: "For Chalmers the new affection was love for God himself. Nothing smaller is strong enough to push the old one out."
5. **Chapter 6, Philippians 3.** "He looked at them beside what he'd found." Name what he found. The verse says "that I may gain Christ".
6. **Chapter 27, Psalm 124.** The bird escapes because of God: the next verse says "Our help is in the LORD's name, who made heaven and earth." Add it.
7. **Chapter 29, Proverbs 24:16.** In context the "righteous man" falls by misfortune, not by sin. It's very commonly applied to moral falls, so leave it, but a teaching pastor may notice.
8. **Chapter 19, James 1:8.** In context "double-minded" is about doubting in prayer. James 4:8, "Purify your hearts, you double-minded", is about divided loyalty and fits better.
9. **Chapter 14, the church guy.** "He has confessed it so many times... that he's embarrassed to bring it up with God again." The chapter leaves this hanging. Add an answer: "Peter once asked Jesus how many times he had to forgive his brother. Seven times? Jesus said seventy times seven. God doesn't hold himself to a lower standard than the one he gave Peter."
10. **Chapter 22, alcohol.** "A man coming off alcohol has a few odd mornings too." Heavy drinkers can have dangerous withdrawal. Make it "A man coming off a habit has a few odd mornings too."
11. **Chapter 22, Romans 7:17 against James 1:14.** "Whose voice is that?" tells the man the voice "isn't you", using "it is no more I that do it". Fit for Service then says, "the appetite is yours". Add half a sentence to Chapter 22: "That doesn't make it someone else's fault. It means you don't have to obey it."
12. **Chapter 28, the non-praying version.** "That is the whole of the final step, and it counts in full." A pastor will ask, counts for what? Try: "As a decision, it counts in full."
13. **Chapter 9, "A man who needs nothing from a woman".** It sits oddly beside "It is not good for the man to be alone" (Chapter 5). Try "A man who isn't hungry to take anything from a woman is the first man who can treat her as a person."
14. **Tone.** Chapter 11 uses "liturgy" and "rite" as insults for the NoFap counter. Liturgical churches will wince, so try "ritual" and "routine". In Chapter 29b, the pebble parody's "My accountability partner checks my shoes every Friday" mocks a practice many pastors recommend, which Chapter 29 itself half recommends ("Tell one man"). Chapter 26: "If every visit has you praising the Lord for what you see there" is a joke some will find irreverent. Chapter 33: "If you're an atheist, play along one last time" before the priestly blessing may grate. Consider "If you're not a believer, take it as a stranger wishing you well."
15. **Context rule, missed references.** Lent (Ch 12: "the forty days before Easter, when many Christians give something up"); "my thorn" (Ch 13: Paul's "thorn in the flesh" was a suffering God allowed, never a sin he was told to keep, so it's worth saying so); the Song of Solomon (Ch 13: "a love poem in the Bible"); the tax collector (Ch 14: "a tax collector, a man everyone despised"); "Godly sorrow" (Ch 16: Paul's phrase); Samson's "vow" (Ch 9: name it as the Nazirite vow, a vow of special dedication to God); Psalm 51 (Ch 32: "David's prayer after Nathan confronted him"); "tempted in every way and did not sin" (Ch 22: say it's from the letter to the Hebrews).
16. **Chapter 9, small hyperbole.** "He was the strongest man who ever lived" and "No army ever beat King David" are fine for storytelling. A pedant might point to Absalom's revolt, when David fled his own city. Leave them unless you want to be safe ("the strongest man in the Bible").
17. **WEB punctuation.** Five quotations differ from the WEB text I checked by a comma or a semicolon. They sound the same aloud. Matthew 6:33 (WEB: "God's Kingdom, and his righteousness"); 1 John 1:9 ("the sins, and to cleanse"); Job 31:1 ("with my eyes, how then"); Galatians 6:1 ("gentleness; looking"); Romans 6:14 ("over you. For you are"). The WEB has had small revisions, so check these against the edition you cite before changing anything.

## Scripture check

I checked about 95 quotations against the World English Bible text (book by book, verse by verse). Every quotation is the right verse and says what the book says it says. None is misquoted in wording. The only differences are the five commas in Low 17.

Stories checked against the text:

- **David, Bathsheba and Uriah** (Ch 5, 9, 23, Fit for Service; 2 Samuel 11 and 12, Uriah among the mighty men in 23:39). Faithful. Details right: the season of war, the roof, Uriah sleeping at the door, the drunk night, the letter carried by Uriah, the "sword devours" reply, Nathan's lamb, the child's death, the sword never leaving his house. Only M5 and M7 apply.
- **Samson and Delilah** (Ch 9; Judges 13 and 16, Hebrews 11:32). Faithful. The cords, the ropes, "pressed him daily", the shaving, "he didn't know that Yahweh had departed from him", the mill, "the hair... began to grow again", Dagon, the boy leading him, the prayer for vengeance "for my two eyes", the death, Hebrews 11. The book is honest that it was a prayer for revenge. Good.
- **Ahab, Jezebel and Naboth** (Ch 9; 1 Kings 16, 21 and 22, 2 Kings 9, Revelation 2:20). Faithful. "Sold himself... whom Jezebel his wife stirred up", the sackcloth, God holding back the disaster, Micaiah imprisoned, the random arrow, Jezebel's death. Only M6 applies. "Being sorry is not the same as turning around" is excellent, and pastors will quote it.
- **The prodigal son** (Introduction, Ch 28; Luke 15). Faithful. "Just come" in Chapter 28 is clearly one preacher's imagining, and is told that way.
- **Adam hiding** (Ch 8; Genesis 3), **Joseph and Potiphar's wife** (Ch 26; Genesis 39), **Peter's denial and "establish your brothers"** (Ch 30; Luke 22), **the Pharisee and the tax collector** (Ch 14; Luke 18), **Nehemiah's fifty-two days** (Ch 9; Nehemiah 6:15), **Isaiah's call** (Fit for Service; see M9). All faithful.

Attributions checked: Psalm 4 and Psalm 32 to David, Psalm 90 to Moses, Proverbs 25 to Solomon (Proverbs 25:1), and Solomon as the son of David and Bathsheba (2 Samuel 12:24). All right.

Proof-texting, where the verse is right but its meaning is stretched: John 8:32 (H2), Matthew 5:34 (M1), Matthew 5:45 (M8), and mildly Proverbs 24:16 and James 1:8 (Low 7 and 8). Philippians 3:7 and Song of Solomon 2:15 are used the way preachers have always used them, and that's fine.

Other quotations a pastor might check: the Screwtape lines, the Lewis letter of 1956, Chalmers's "expulsive power of a new affection", Augustine's "only not yet" and the Benedictine "Marry a man who can fast". All are accurate.

## What to keep (pastors will like these)

- Romans 8:1 set beside the call to repent, from the Introduction on. "The word 'sin' is here to tell the truth. It is not here to beat you."
- Lust defined as "desire turned into taking", and desire called God's idea. This matches Catholic teaching on chastity and Protestant teaching alike.
- "It's not a sin to be tempted", and wet dreams named as no sin. Both traditions agree.
- Chapter 2's "our fault and not our fault at the same time". The Catholic Catechism (2352) says that the "force of acquired habit" can lessen how guilty a man is, so this is on solid ground.
- The Pharisee and the tax collector closing Chapter 14.
- The prayer in Chapter 28: confession, sorrow, forgiveness, a resolution, and "I can't keep this by my own strength... Keep me."
- "Being fit is not the price of being loved. It is what a loved man is for."
- The wives' section, the abuse section and the crisis section. These are the parts a pastor most fears a self-help book will get wrong, and they're careful and right.
- Chapter 29b's "Wanting it to hurt" (with M12's fix) and "Repentance is quick and it faces forward."
