# The Hydra {#the-hydra}

> "But each one is tempted when he is drawn away by his own lust and enticed. Then the lust, when it has conceived, bears sin."
>
> — James 1:14-15

The ancient Greeks told a story about a monster called the Hydra.

It lived in a swamp, and it had many heads. The trouble with it was this. Cut off one head, and two grew back in its place. The harder a man fought it, the more heads he had to fight.

You know this monster. You have been fighting it for years.

You swore off porn, and for three weeks you were clean. Then you noticed you were spending an hour a night scrolling past women in swimsuits and calling it nothing. One head came off, and two grew back. Or you left the phone in the kitchen at bedtime, and the reel in your memory started up in its place. Or you stopped the whole business by force, and poured the same hunger into chasing a woman who didn't want you. Every time you cut, it grew.

No wonder it feels unbeatable. No wonder you decided the fault was in you.

So let's count the heads. The old storytellers never agreed on the number. Ours has five, and you can count them on one hand.

**Look.** Porn is the big one, in every form it comes in. But the feed full of half-dressed strangers belongs here too. So does the second glance at a woman in the street, the one you take on purpose.

**Think.** The private film. The fantasy, the memory played back, the woman from work undressed in your head.

**Touch.** The hand. For most men this is the head that has bitten most often. It is on the cover of this book.

**Chase.** This is lust doing your thinking around real women. The flirting you shouldn't be doing. The message you shouldn't be sending. The woman on a pedestal, and the money spent to make her notice you.

**Take.** Sex outside marriage. The one-night stand, the affair, the sex a man pays for. And the one nobody likes to hear: the woman he loves and means to marry someday.

Look, think, touch, chase, take. It is a ladder, and each rung leads to the next. It is also the oldest story there is. He saw her. He couldn't stop thinking about her. He went after her. He took her.

You may know all five heads, or only two. It makes no difference, and the story explains why.

A hero named Hercules was sent to kill the Hydra. He was the strongest man in all the Greek stories, and he began the way everybody begins. He swung, a head fell, and two grew. Strength got him nowhere. More strength got him nowhere faster.

What saved him was his nephew, a young man named Iolaus, who came running with a burning torch. Each time Hercules took off a head, Iolaus held the fire to the stump. Nothing grows from a burned stump. One by one, the heads stopped coming back.

Notice three things.

First, the sword was never going to work. The sword is willpower. It is every vow you made, every streak you counted, every app you deleted in disgust. You didn't swing too weakly. Cutting is what makes the heads grow.

Second, the answer was light. A head grows back because something under it is still alive. In you, that something is a belief: that this head had something to give you. Leave the belief alive and you can cut all day. Burn it, and there is nothing left to grow from. That is all this book does. It holds a light to each stump until you see what was under it. And a man doesn't have to be strong to hold a torch.

Third, he didn't do it alone. The strongest man in the story needed somebody to hand him the fire. So do you, and there is no shame in that. Christians have a word for the fire you didn't light yourself. They call it grace.

Now here is something the old story leaves out. By firelight you can finally see what you have been fighting. All those necks go down to one body. Only one. And for all its heads and all its hissing, the body is small. The Hydra looked enormous to every man with a sword, because he never once saw past the heads. The legend was written by men who had only ever cut.

Five heads, and one small body feeding through all of them. That is why you are not going to fight five battles.

One head, the story says, could not be killed. Hercules cut it off and buried it under a great rock beside the road. It could hiss down there for as long as it liked. It could bite nobody. You'll want to remember that rock.

But before we light anything, one question has to be settled. Five heads make a long list. Is all of that really lust? Where exactly is the line?
