The Author applies fixes once James decides. Locations are given by chapter and short quoted phrase, as they stand on main today; if the Author's current pass has already reworded a line, the concern may already be fixed.

## 1. What the clinicians say the book gets right (protect these)

All three agreed on most of this list:
- **Slip handling is close to textbook relapse prevention.** Ch 29b ("no zero to go back to", "Refuse the second one", the "may as well" thought) and the counter critique in Ch 11 ("the counter turned one bad night into three") target the abstinence-violation effect, the main way one lapse becomes a binge.
- **Urges are accepted, not fought.** "A feeling is not an order" and "Let it ring" (Ch 22, Ch 28), "Don't try not to think about it". This matches urge surfing, ACT and the thought-suppression research. The CBT clinician calls it the best clinical material in the book.
- **Shame is treated as fuel.** "Guilt loads the gun", and not calling a teen filthy (Ch 30).
- **Ch 5 works as a functional analysis** of triggers (stress, boredom, sleep, loneliness, rejection), and "It's about emotional regulation" matches the coping-motives research.
- **The "not a relapse" list protects scrupulous and young readers**: temptation as weather, wet dreams "not a sin, and not a slip", morning erections as plumbing, "Noticing that a woman is beautiful is not a fall", "Wanting it to hurt" (Ch 22, 29b). The sex therapist calls the wet-dream line the most protective sentence in the book.
- **It is honest about the body and brain.** It leaves out dopamine and "rewiring" claims entirely ("some of it is shaky, and you don't need any of it", Ch 3), debunks semen retention and the retracted testosterone study accurately, frames death grip as "a link and no more" with "see a doctor", and says plainly that "porn addiction" is "a moral and spiritual judgment, not a medical one" (Ch 4).
- **The wives' section is mostly sound**: "not your fault", one whole disclosure rather than drips, "your no is yours", safety first, no "co-addict" label (Ch 31).
- **Safety signposting** on trauma, child abuse material, suicide and sextortion is good, and "Never ignore your doctor" (Rule 4) is right.
- **The safety facts check out** where verified: the GirlsDoPorn sentence, the retraction, the FBI sextortion release.

## 2. Concerns, ranked

Ranked by how legitimate (clinical consensus beats reasonable view beats opinion) and how important (who could be harmed). **[H/M/L]** is severity. **[CC]** clinical consensus, **[RV]** reasonable clinical view.

### Tier 1: could hurt someone, or false

**1. [H][CC] Depression is read as a failure to rejoice, and warning signs are told to "wait it out".** *(CBT, sex therapist, researcher all raised it)*
- **Where:** Ch 21 "Don't mope about it. Rejoice." and "a man who mopes for three weeks will be moping in the fourth"; Ch 22 "If you catch yourself feeling low, remember the habit was causing the lowness" and the flatline section ("felt depressed... It passed", "If it happens, leave it alone"); Ch 28 "Are you excited?" as a condition of the final step; Ch 29 "I'm miserable and irritable" answered with "something hasn't clicked"; Ch 22 and the FAQ "check that everything still works" as "the one mistake"; Ch 8 "the body, like the lawn, grows back".
- **Why:** Low mood and loss of interest are core symptoms of depression, which is common in men with compulsive sexual behaviour. A depressed man cannot make himself feel glad on cue, and the book gives him two readings of his mood (it proves he hasn't "got it", or the habit is dying), neither of which sends him to a doctor. The flatline concept comes from an advocacy site (yourbrainonporn.com), not clinical research, and "generally temporary" was said about recovery in general. Erection trouble, especially over 40, can be an early sign of heart disease or diabetes; low desire with low mood needs screening for depression, low testosterone, thyroid and medication effects. Telling a man not to notice whether things work removes the information a doctor asks for.
- **Fix:** In the flatline paragraph (Ch 22), in Ch 29 and in the Appendix: "If you feel low most of the day for more than two weeks, lose interest in things you used to enjoy, can't sleep or eat, or if low desire or erection trouble lasts more than a few weeks, that isn't the Little Monster. See a doctor. It's very treatable, and erection trouble can be an early warning about your heart, especially after forty. You don't have to feel glad for this to work." Change "grows back" to "often recovers". Make "Are you excited?" optional ("Gladness may come later").

**2. [H][CC] No guidance for OCD or scrupulosity, and several tools can feed it.** *(CBT, sex therapist)*
- **Where:** Ch 15 "You always know which kind you're doing" and "In his head he has already said yes" and "Faithful only because nobody offered"; Ch 28 "Are you certain?... If you have doubts... re-read the book first"; Ch 29b confess every slip to God, your wife and the man you told; Ch 22 "Don't lie awake... replaying the dream either. That part you are awake for"; Ch 13 "See thousands of locked doors".
- **Why:** A man with religious or sexual OCD can't tell a "second look" from an intrusive thought; that is the disorder. "You always know" triggers endless checking; a required certainty before the final step is a trap with no exit for someone whose illness is doubt; confessing every slip becomes a reassurance ritual. Fantasies about other people are near-universal and don't predict infidelity, and "faithful only because nobody offered" is heads-I-win.
- **Fix:** A short box (Ch 15 or near the final step): "If you get stuck checking whether a look or a thought was a sin, read this book differently. If you can't tell, treat it as noticing and move on. Don't confess the same thing twice. You will never feel certain, so don't wait to be. Ask a doctor about ERP (exposure and response prevention), the treatment that works for this." Make the final-step check "Have you decided?", not "Are you certain?" Soften "always know" to "most men know". For Catholics the tradition's own remedy fits: a scrupulous penitent follows one confessor's direction. *(Removing the danger checklist, as METHOD-CHECK asks, also helps here.)*

**3. [H][CC] Unwanted intrusive thoughts are lumped with dangerous interest.** *(CBT)*
- **Where:** Introduction and Appendix: "Your urges frighten you, or what you look at has moved toward children."
- **Why:** The referral is right. But many men with OCD have horrifying, unwanted intrusive thoughts about children or other repellent acts and never act on them. In a book about sin, this line can confirm their fear that they are dangerous, and fear raises distress and suicide risk. Unwanted intrusions and growing real interest in illegal material are different problems.
- **Fix:** Keep it and add: "If thoughts like this horrify you, keep popping up uninvited, and you would never act on them, that is often a known, treatable anxiety problem (OCD), not a sign you are dangerous. Tell a doctor exactly that."

**4. [H][CC] A treatment source is quoted for the opposite of what it says (death grip).** *(sex therapist, researcher; verified against the author's source notes)*
- **Where:** Ch 8 "notice what the specialists prescribe... They tell these men to stop"; Ch 29 "the restraint he asks of them is 'only temporary'", and "several hundred young men" (the study had 448 men).
- **Why:** The cited source (Perelman) prescribes a *temporary* pause followed by *retraining* how the man masturbates, often with couple exercises. The book borrows his authority for permanent abstinence. A man could refuse the real treatment when a sex therapist assigns it.
- **Fix:** "Specialists who treat this usually ask a man to pause for a while and then change how he does it. This book asks you to stop for good. That's my conviction, not their prescription. If a sex therapist gives you exercises, that is treatment, so talk it through with them." Check "several hundred" against the study's 448.

**5. [H][RV] Disclosure to a wife is scripted as cheerful certainty, which can set up a second betrayal.** *(sex therapist)*
- **Where:** Ch 28 "Tell it as a free man giving news: 'This had me for years. It's over.'"; Ch 29b "tell your wife as well" after a slip.
- **Why:** To a betrayed wife, "It's over" said brightly sounds like minimising and makes a promise; if he slips (which the book plans for), she experiences a fresh deception, and repeated broken promises predict worse trauma than the original discovery. Missing in plain words: STI testing when there was an affair or paid sex, and therapist-facilitated disclosure. Some wives want every slip told; others find it retraumatising, and for a scrupulous man it becomes a ritual.
- **Fix:** Keep the friend script and give the wife a separate one: "I've hidden this from you for years. I've stopped, and I'll tell you if that ever changes." Add: "If there was another person or paid sex, both of you get tested before you are intimate again. A counselor can help you plan the disclosure." In 29b: "Agree with your wife, ideally with a counselor, what she wants to be told."

**6. [H][RV] The method is unfalsifiable and failure is always the reader's fault.** *(CBT, researcher)*
- **Where:** Introduction "only two ways to fail... not following... not understanding"; Ch 26 and Ch 29 "something hasn't clicked"; Ch 11/12 successes by other methods happen "in spite of" them; Ch 22 every discomfort is "the Little Monster dying"; Ch 24 "the method still works" for trauma and depression.
- **Why:** Every outcome confirms the model (success proves it, discomfort proves it, failure means you got it wrong). That hurts most the men the book wants to reach (depressed, traumatised, ADHD, scrupulous), who will conclude "the method is perfect, so I am the defect", and it stops a man from concluding "I need different help".
- **Fix:** One honest exit near "two ways to fail" and in Ch 29: "If you have followed all of this, read it twice, and you are still struggling after a couple of months, that doesn't mean you misunderstood. Some men need a counselor as well. That's the next step, not a defeat." Change "only two ways to fail" to "the two commonest reasons". Soften "the method still works" (Ch 24) to "the book can still help". **Method-sensitive** (it is the Carr "you can't fail" posture); the exit line keeps the confidence and removes the trap.

**7. [H][RV] The "addiction" and "master" language makes every reader feel hooked, which the research links to distress.** *(all three)*
- **Where:** Introduction "If you said yes to even one of these, keep reading"; Ch 14 the once-a-fortnight man is "more firmly caught than the man who does it daily"; Ch 2 "IT'S A MASTER, NOT A HABIT" and "carried this habit to the grave without once suspecting"; "crack" and "the same drug in a smaller glass" (Ch 4, 11, 12, 21, 26); "withdrawal", "sobering up".
- **Why:** In many samples, believing "I'm addicted" is predicted more by moral disapproval and religiosity than by how much a man actually uses, and that belief predicts distress and depression on its own (Grubbs and colleagues; ICD-11 excludes distress that comes only from moral judgment). Ch 4 says this honestly, then the rest of the book undoes it. Men with truly compulsive use get no route to treatments that work.
- **Fix:** Keep the sin language (the author's choice). Drop medical-sounding vocabulary where it implies a clinical claim ("crack", "drug", "sobering up"). Change "even one" to "If several of these fit and they trouble you". Cut or soften "more firmly caught" and "to the grave without suspecting". Add near "Is this you?": "Feeling hooked and being compulsive are not the same thing. Many men who feel enslaved use it rarely. Their misery comes from the war, and that is good news."

**8. [H][CC] The referral list is incomplete, and "most men need nothing more" has no data behind it.** *(all three)*
- **Where:** Introduction "Most men need nothing more than these pages"; Appendix "Most readers will never need more".
- **Why:** It discourages help-seeking. Missing: use that costs hours a day, money, job or legal risk (compulsive sexual behaviour); sex combined with alcohol or drugs; new hypersexuality in a bipolar high; medication (dopamine agonists for Parkinson's or restless legs can cause sudden compulsive sexual behaviour); brain injury; ADHD; OCD; persistent sexual dysfunction; sex therapists (also missing from the appendix). The book never says that treatment works (CBT, ACT, sometimes medicine).
- **Fix:** Replace "most" with "many". Add a short bulleted "See a professional as well if..." list in the Appendix and a one-line pointer in the Introduction, including a sex therapist for erection or orgasm problems and for different amounts of desire. Add: "A doctor's instructions override this book."

### Tier 2: false or overstated claims (the researcher's list; most are quick fixes)

**9. [H][CC] "Three in four men who watch it are not compulsive" is wrong (Ch 4).** It traces through a YouTube psychiatrist to Vaillancourt-Morel et al. 2017 (verified): 830 adults, **71.8% women**, 75.5% recreational, **12.7% highly distressed but not compulsive**, 11.8% compulsive. So it is not a figure about men, 88% (not 75%) were non-compulsive, and it leaves out the group the moral-incongruence research predicts. Fix: cite the study: "In one study of adults who watch porn, nearly nine in ten were not compulsive. One in eight used it little but felt great distress about it. If that is you, the trouble may be the war more than the habit, and this book aims to end the war." This also fits the method better.

**10. [M][CC] Ego depletion quoted as fact (Ch 11).** "Willpower is a diminishing resource" failed a 23-lab replication (2016) and a second large one (2021). The book's argument doesn't need it. Cut it, or say "A man fighting all day gets tired."

**11. [M][RV] One psychiatrist reads as several (Ch 4, 5, 11, 22).** "A psychiatrist who treats porn addiction" is the same online psychiatrist in about seven places, and "number one cause of relapse is boredom" is one clinician's talk presented as a ranking. "One coach" has the same pattern. Fix: name or identify once ("a psychiatrist who talks about this online") then "the same psychiatrist", and recast rankings as "in his experience". Same for the coach who sells a course.

**12. [M][RV] Hookup regret is cherry-picked (Ch 4).** The 72% / 78% regret figures are accurate, but the same review reports 82% of men and 57% of women were "generally glad", and half of men felt positive afterwards. Fix: cut, or give fairly: "Studies are mixed. Many report feeling fine, but most who had casual sex said they regretted it at least once."

**13. [M][RV] AI-companion statistic is attached to the wrong group (Ch 14).** The 31% of young men is right; "one in five liked it better" is 21% of *everyone who had tried* an AI partner (verified in the Wheatley/IFS summary). Fix: "of everyone in the survey who had tried it, about one in five". Also label the deepfake count (95,820 videos) as "one industry report" (Security Hero, a company marketing report, now dated), and update the FBI sextortion figures ("and the numbers have grown since").

**14. [M][RV] The death-grip and flatline sourcing** are covered in items 1 and 4. Add the researcher's smaller points: "the flatline" is an advocacy-site concept; "Dr Weiss: 80% abuse survivors" is a clinic figure (the book rightly says "most of the men who come to him").

**15. [M][RV] Bodily claims stated as fact in a book that says it is not about the body (Ch 2, 3, 5).** "Masturbation doesn't calm a man down. It winds him up." "It was the habit that kept you awake." "Food feeds you. This drains you." The evidence, though modest, points the other way on sleep (orgasm is reported to help many people sleep; higher ejaculation frequency is linked to lower prostate-cancer risk). Fix: frame as the man's observation: "Many men find it helps them drop off. What it can't give you is the sleep of a man with nothing on his mind. Lie there a few nights and see."

**16. [M][RV] The Carr nicotine model is carried to sex without saying so, and "no pleasure at all" is the weakest empirical claim (Ch 1, 3, 11, 18).** "There are no benefits. Not one." "Enjoyment has nothing to do with this." Masturbation has no pharmacological withdrawal, and orgasm is a primary reward. The same overclaim ("yes there is a moment of pleasure") invites ridicule from a sceptical reader. Fix: keep the moral claim ("it gives you nothing worth having"), drop the factual one: "Yes, there is a moment of pleasure. Look at what it costs and what follows, and ask whether you would choose it." **Method-sensitive** (the "no benefits" illusion chain is the book's heart); the reworded version keeps the chain and is harder to refute.

**17. [M][RV] Invented timelines and universal statements (Ch 27, 11, Introduction, 2, 18).** "About five days in... most men have their moment", "About three weeks in is when most willpower attempts die", "Many more fail than succeed that way", "Most men need nothing more", "not one would choose it again", "Every man who still has it wishes he'd never begun". No data supports them (the day counts are Carr's nicotine timeline), and a reader who doesn't fit is told he is lying (Ch 14). Fix: "many men report", "in the testimonies I read", and cut "most" where there is no figure. Put the Ch 27 "rough guide" caveat before the numbers, not after. (The Author's current promise-softening pass may cover part of this.)

**18. [M][CC] Urge speed and permanence are overpromised, against extinction research.** "Grass grows back" (Ch 3), "fades after a minute or two" (Ch 22), "a few restless evenings", "nothing in the body lasts a year" (Ch 5), while Ch 3 also admits the path is "still there waiting". Extinction is new learning laid over the old: old cues revive urges months or years later, especially on a bad day or in an old place. A man told his urge lasts two minutes, and that strong urges after months mean the lie is back, will read a normal return as failure. Fix: "Urges rise and fall like a wave, usually over minutes. Old cues can wake them months or years later, especially on a bad day or in an old place. That's normal. It means nothing, and it passes the same way." Pick one account of how durable the path is (Ch 3 contradicts itself).

### Tier 3: reasonable concerns for particular readers

**19. [M][RV] "Just once does not exist" teaches the one-means-all belief that turns a slip into a binge (Ch 13, 29).** Said four times in capitals, then Ch 29b repairs it in one short chapter; the main text works against it. Fix: keep the rule and drop the catastrophe: "One time is never harmless, and it is never the end. It doesn't ruin anything. It only tempts you to make it two." Point forward to Ch 29b. **Method-sensitive** (a signature Easy Way line); the rewrite keeps the line but removes the doom.

**20. [M][RV] No teaching for what to do with sadness, and a ban on every substitute (Ch 25 "USE NONE OF THEM", Ch 1 "no benefits", Ch 5 "I don't know how to handle being sad").** For men using it to regulate emotion (trauma, depression, ADHD, loneliness), the need is real, and CBT and ACT work by meeting it healthily. Fix: separate substitutes from needs: "Sadness, stress and loneliness are real and need looking after: sleep, a walk, a friend, prayer, telling someone. These aren't substitutes for lust. They are what the habit was falsely promising." One paragraph in Ch 5 or 25.

**21. [M][RV] Shame by character verdict, against the book's own promise.** "A man who can't control his lust can't be trusted" in bold (Introduction, Ch 9, Ch 21, Ch 30), "He looks like a burglar", "Faithful only because nobody offered", "unfit for service", "pigs eat trash". Guilt (about the act) motivates repair; shame (about the self) motivates hiding and relapse, as the book itself says. Fix: make it about the behaviour and about time: "While lust is making his decisions, a man can't fully trust his own word. That changes the day it stops." Cut "pigs eat trash". **Method-sensitive** (it is a thread the author built), but it is the strongest legitimate shame concern.

**22. [M][RV] Trauma: stopping can bring things up.** Referrals are good, but "the method still works" (Ch 24) and every urge being "the Little Monster" leave out that stopping a numbing behaviour can release flashbacks, nightmares, panic or dissociation. Fix (Ch 22): "If stopping brings up memories, nightmares, panic or numbness, that isn't the Little Monster. It is the old wound surfacing. Tell a trauma counselor; it is a sign you need a person alongside the book."

**23. [M][RV] Teens and parents.** The boy-facing text frames puberty-onset masturbation as a life-defining trap ("a fly... on the rim of the jar", Ch 14; "the worst thing that happened to them", Ch 17), and parents are told to present it as a trap (Ch 31 "this habit hijacks it"). Early heavy sexual shame is linked to later anxiety and sexual difficulty. "If you're under eighteen, tell a parent" (Ch 29) may bring punishment in a strict home. Fix: add "What your body is doing is normal and you are not broken or dirty", soften "fly on the rim", add to the parent list "Tell him his body is good. Erections and wet dreams are normal and not sins. If he does it, he hasn't ruined anything, and he can always talk to you", and change "a parent" to "a parent or another adult you feel safe with". Open question: the site is public, so 14-year-olds will read it. Does the author want a short section addressed to boys directly?

**24. [M][RV] Wives told how to feel; other explanations for bedroom distance shut out (Ch 31).** "It is a betrayal, and you may call it that" (Ch 30 says "may be"); "If he has seemed far away in the bedroom, that came from the habit too" rules out depression, erection trouble, performance anxiety, medication and relationship problems. Fix: "It may feel like a betrayal, and if it does, you may call it that." "...the habit may be part of it. A doctor or counselor can help you both find the rest."

**25. [M][RV] Desire mismatch in marriage, and the scope of "done with masturbation" (Ch 14 married man, Ch 22, Ch 6).** Solo sex is often how couples manage a gap in desire (pregnancy, illness, postpartum). Taking it away and promising he'll "get it back" can turn into pressure on her; the book says her tiredness is "never his excuse" but doesn't warn him against pressing her. "I am done with masturbation", undefined, will make some husbands refuse or feel guilty about ordinary touch during married sex. Fix: "Stopping must never become pressure on her. If you find yourself pushing her more, that is lust using another door." In the definitions: "'The habit' means sex on your own. What a husband and wife do together is theirs to decide."

**26. [M][RV] Gay and bisexual men, and orientation (Ch 3, 6, 11, 28).** The book is silent, and "you simply won't want them", "neither the need nor the wish" can be read as a promise of orientation change; attempts at that are linked to depression and suicidality. This does not touch the author's sexual ethic. Fix: one sentence: "This book is about lust making your decisions. It doesn't promise to change who you are attracted to, and it isn't a tool for that. If anyone has promised to change your orientation, or you feel hopeless about it, talk to a counselor and see the crisis section."

**27. [L-M][CC] "Not once, for any reason" collides with medical need (Ch 28, 29b).** A semen analysis, sex-therapy retraining and some pelvic-pain plans need ejaculation; couples facing infertility will freeze. Fix: "A doctor's request, for a fertility test or a treatment plan, is medicine, not a slip. If it troubles your conscience, your pastor or priest will have met it before." (Watch the Catholic-reader wording.)

**28. [L][RV] Smaller items.**
- Escalation stated as a law ("then harder porn", Ch 3, 14, 12): evidence is mixed. "Many men" and "the line often moves". (Also feeds checking in men with OCD.)
- After a long gap sex may be quicker than he'd like (Ch 12 "Fireworks"): add one reassuring line so it doesn't become performance anxiety.
- "No apps" in the Introduction can be read as banning blockers that help ADHD: in Ch 26 add "If blockers help you, they are sense, not a gimmick."
- "Habits change in a week" (Ch 2): habit research says a median of about 66 days. Harmless rhetoric but wrong as written.
- "Married men do it an hour after making love" (Ch 3) as proof of no enjoyment can reflect delayed orgasm or desire mismatch.
- The 30 minutes a day arithmetic (Ch 7) assumes daily use; most men don't.
- Testimonials are self-selected: add "These are the men who wrote in. Plenty didn't."
- "Porn is the crack": the pharmacology claim and the racial baggage of the crack-era coverage. Cut or say "cheap and everywhere".
- "Somebody always does [get hurt]" and "In every country police keep finding women" (Ch 4) overreach: "Often somebody does, and you can't tell which time."

## 3. Objections that come only from a worldview (noted, not recommended)

These came up from the clinical readers' own frames; I list them so the author knows they were considered and left alone:
- That calling lust "sin" or reading Matthew 5:28 as covering fantasy is itself the harm. (A secular sex-therapist or sex-positive view. The book chooses this position.)
- That masturbation is normal and healthy for everyone and quitting it is unnecessary. (The book's chosen stance; the honest "doctors disagree" line already stands.)
- That sex reserved for marriage should go, or be softened for single men.
- That the Easy Way's "no cutting down" should allow moderation. (It would blur the book; the sex-positive avatar in `READER-REPORT-2.md` said so too.)
- That porn is not prostitution and casual sex is not "using". (The book's moral claim.)
- Dropping the Christian frame or the prayer.

Items 6, 16, 19 and 21 touch lines that are part of the Easy Way's signature (you can't fail; no benefits; just once does not exist; the trust thread). ## 5. Questions only the author or a real clinician can settle

1. **Keep the word "addiction"?** It is used while disclaiming a medical claim, which is the central inconsistency (item 7).
2. **Teen readers.** Is the book meant for under 16s? If yes, a short section addressed to boys, reviewed by an adolescent clinician.
3. **A real clinical review** of the proposed OCD box and certainty-check wording (ideally an ERP-trained clinician familiar with religious scrupulosity), the sexual-function box, and the referral list, before launch.
4. **Outcome data.** Will the author collect any (for example an anonymous follow-up in the site feedback), so claims like "has freed many men" and "most men need nothing more" rest on something?
5. **Scope inside marriage and medical exceptions** (items 25 and 27), including how the exception reads for Catholic readers.
6. **How long** should a man give the method before the book tells him to add professional help? A concrete figure (for example eight weeks) would replace the open-ended "re-read".
7. **Disclosure.** Should the book recommend therapist-facilitated disclosure for affairs and paid sex, and how much should a man tell about solo masturbation with no porn and no other person?
8. **Same-sex-attracted readers.** What does the author want such a reader to take from the book (item 26)?

## 6. What was and wasn't verified

- **Verified on the web:** the Vaillancourt-Morel 2017 clusters; the Wheatley/IFS AI-companion figures; the APA Monitor hookup-regret figures; the GirlsDoPorn sentence (27 years, September 2025); the retraction of the 2003 testosterone study; the ego-depletion replication failures.
- **Checked against the author's source notes only:** the death-grip case-control study (448 men); the FBI sextortion release; the OnlyFans and Reuters "chatters" figures; the Security Hero deepfake report; the Pornhub removals; the Lewis, Chalmers and Maltz quotes.
- **Not verifiable as stated:** the "five days" and "three weeks" timelines; "most men need nothing more"; "many more fail than succeed"; the universal regret statements.
